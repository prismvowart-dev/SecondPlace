# Chapter 34: Voluntary

It took him four days to decide and four more to get his leg to agree to the walk, and on the eighth of the fourteen days he had, he told Kaito he was going to the administration building and that he was going alone.

Kaito came anyway, which Itsuki had expected, and did not argue about it, which he had not.

"I'm not signing anything," Kaito said, falling into step at the pace the crutches allowed, slower than either of them was used to. "I'm just going to be in the building. In case you need someone to shout at a clerk. I've had practice."

"I don't need you to shout at anyone."

"I know. I'm bringing the option."

The walk took nineteen minutes instead of the six it should have, and by the end of it his shoulders had the dull ache of arms doing a leg's job longer than they had agreed to. He was almost grateful. It gave him something to feel that was not the thing he had come to feel.

The window was the one Kaito had described in December, set into a wall of frosted glass with a brass slot at the bottom and a strip of tape on the counter marking where to stand. The clerk behind it looked like someone who had said no to a great many people that month and had enjoyed none of it.

"I need the form for voluntary withdrawal," Itsuki said.

She looked up. Her expression changed when she matched his face to whatever was on her screen. "You're the one from the hardship letter."

"I am."

"You have six days before the payment deadline. You don't have to do this yet."

"I know what happens on day fourteen," he said. "I'd rather choose the day than have it chosen."

She studied him as though the transaction had a second page, and he thought she might argue, tell him to wait, to hope for something to change in six days that had not changed in six weeks. She did not. She reached under the counter and came back with a form he knew by its weight before he had read a word of it, the flat gray of a paper that exists only to end things.

"Sections one to four. Section five needs a witness signature if you're not accompanied by a guardian."

"I've got that," Kaito said from the bench behind him, before anyone had asked.

A seal and a signature in front of a registrar. Everything the exemption had needed. It had taken him twenty-three days past the deadline to be able to stand here. He set the thought down and picked up the pen.

The form took longer than it should have, not because it was complicated but because his hand kept trying to write the version of his life in which the form did not exist. Name. Student number. Reason for withdrawal, a single line. After a moment he wrote *financial hardship following medical leave*, five words doing the work of six weeks, and it struck him as the most honest sentence he had set down in his time at Koyo.

He signed at the bottom with the signature he had used on every form for two and a half years. It looked exactly as it always had, and that seemed strange to him, that something this large could end in ink that had not changed.

Kaito signed the witness line without being asked twice and said nothing while he did it, which Itsuki took as a kind of respect.

He slid the form back. The clerk checked each section, then stamped the corner with more gentleness than the stamp needed.

"It's processed as of today. You'll get a confirmation letter in five to seven working days." She hesitated. "I'm sorry it came to this."

"Thank you for the form."

It was not the answer she had expected, he could tell, but she nodded anyway, and that was the whole transaction. Two and a half years, reduced to a sheet of gray paper and a stamp that took less than a second to land.

___

Outside, the cold had teeth. Kaito kept pace beside him toward the dormitory and for the first minutes neither of them spoke, which for Kaito was a tribute.

"You're leaving Tuesday," Kaito said at last, carefully. "And you haven't told her."

"I'll tell her when it's done."

"It is done. I watched the stamp."

"When it's done done. The room. The bus." Itsuki adjusted his grip on the crutch. "If I tell her before, she writes a clause."

"Maybe you should let her write the clause."

Itsuki did not answer, which Kaito accepted as an answer, and they walked on.

"How do you feel?" Kaito asked, a little further on, as if he were not sure the question was allowed yet.

Itsuki gave it the honest consideration he did not usually give anyone, himself included.

"Lighter," he said. "I didn't expect that." He hated, a little, that it was true.

"Yeah, well." Kaito bumped a shoulder against his, careful of the crutch. "Nobody tells you that quitting a fight you were never going to win counts as something. Nobody puts it on a poster."

"It's not winning."

"No," Kaito agreed. "But it's yours. That's different, and I think it's the part that's going to matter in a year."

Itsuki did not answer that either. The two of them walked the rest of the way in a quiet that had stopped needing to be filled.
