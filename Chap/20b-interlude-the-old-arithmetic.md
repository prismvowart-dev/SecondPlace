# Interlude: The Old Arithmetic

Her mother called on a Sunday, which meant it was not a casual call. Saionji Miho did not telephone casually. She scheduled, as she scheduled everything, and Sunday evening was the slot reserved for whatever this was going to be.

Reina took it on the mat, sitting cross-legged with her back against the wall bars and a towel still around her neck. She had finished the block twenty minutes earlier. The log lay open beside her knee: thirty-five, thirty-three, thirty-one.

"Are you having fun," her mother said.

It was not a question. There was no rising note at the end of it, no room left for an answer. It was the tone Reina had spent a lifetime learning to translate.

"That's what your father says when I ask him why you've been in second place since May. He says you're enjoying yourself. Making friends." A pause, placed as carefully as a scalpel. "I would like to know how long you intend to keep enjoying it."

"The seminar track runs on different metrics from the standard..."

"I am not asking about metrics. I am asking how long my daughter intends to sit in second place and call it something else." Her mother's voice didn't rise. It never rose. Rising would have meant losing a degree of control, and control was the one thing Miho never misplaced, not even by accident. "Your grandfather held first in his cohort for four consecutive years. I held it for three. You held first in every cohort you were ever ranked in, until Koyo. I would like to understand what changed."

"Nothing has changed in how I work, Mother."

"Then the explanation is somewhere else." A pause. "How long do you intend to remain in second place, Reina? Or shall I have that room cleared now, and spare us both March?"

Reina looked at the log. Then the mirror, the parallettes, the rack bolted to the wall. The room had taken a negotiation and four years to become hers.

"It isn't that I'm not trying."

"I didn't suggest it was."

"The person in first place is a monster." She said it evenly, the way she would have given a figure. "I'm not saying it to excuse myself. I'm telling you what I am measuring against. I hope you will meet him in person, someday."

The silence on the line was a different length from the ones before it. "A monster," her mother said. "I see. Then I look forward to the introduction." A beat. "That does not answer my question."

"It does." Reina took the towel from her neck and folded it in half. "You won't need to clear the room. I'll stop training. I won't touch any of it until I'm first again. As we agreed."

"As we agreed," her mother said, and in her voice, for the length of the three words, was something that was not satisfaction and was not its opposite.

The call ended the way her mother's calls always ended: efficiently, with no goodbye that resembled warmth, because warmth had never been the currency in that house. Expectation was the currency. It always had been. Reina had simply spent the autumn forgetting to keep track of the exchange rate.

___

She sat very still for a while after the line went dead, in the kind of stillness that has nothing to do with calm.

Then she got up. She did not train. She stood at the center of the mat with her arms at her sides, and the mirror showed a gray room with a person in it, upright, level, no wasted motion. She took the pen, wrote the date in the log under thirty-one, and added a single word. *Suspended.*

She put the log on the shelf beside her glasses, turned off the light, and closed the door. It was the only door in the house that closed without a sound.

___

She remembered, afterward, the first exam she had ever lost to him.

It had been May, before the library table belonged to anyone but her, before a dictionary had needed sharing, when Shindou Itsuki had been nothing in her accounting but a rounding error the university had made in her favor and then, inexplicably, unmade. She had lost by four points. She had gone to the library alone and stayed until the building nearly locked her in, running the same three problems until the numbers stopped meaning anything, because doing something with her hands had always been easier than the alternative.

*I will not lose again*, she had told herself, more than once. More often than she was proud of.

She had lost again. Several times since, in ways too regular to be coincidence. It was the part she could not make the numbers say anything about: not that he tried to unsettle her, but that he had never once appeared to try at all.

___

The Harrington Prize was open to research-track students this year, the first year it had been. She had not thought about it in weeks. She had a research partner and a notebook and a routine that had quietly stopped requiring her to keep score, and she saw now that the not-thinking was the thing her mother had just diagnosed over the phone, with the precision of a woman who had never once been wrong about her daughter and did not intend to start.

She opened her planner. Submissions closed on the eighteenth of December. She wrote the date in red, the color she kept for things that would actually be done, and beneath it, smaller, a line she read twice before closing the book.

*First. This time, first.*

She did not write his name anywhere on the page. It took more effort than it should have.
