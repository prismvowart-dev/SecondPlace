# Chapter 27: Anything Different

She came back on a Thursday, which he had known she would, because Yui had said a week and Yui did not deal in approximations.

He saw her before she saw him, at the bottom of the library steps, and spent the three seconds available cataloguing what had changed. Nothing dramatic. The gray blazer, the same bag, the same thin frames. But under her eyes there was a faint, even shadow of the kind that doesn't come from one bad night, and her hair, pinned as it always was, had been brushed with a care that wasn't hers, as if somebody else had done it that morning and she'd let them.

"Shindou-san," she said, when she had climbed to his step. "You're early."

"You're later."

"I had to sign the return form at the office." She looked at the two coffees in his hands. "One of those is mine."

"Tuesday both were."

"I'd like to thank you for the second cup. On Tuesday." She said it to the bannister, in the voice of someone reading an invoice. "Yui mentioned it. I'm sorry for the waste."

He felt his ears go warm and was annoyed by it. "It wasn't waste."

"No." She took the nearer cup from his hand. "Thank you."

___

They climbed to the third floor in silence, past Mori at the desk, who gave them the nod of a woman who had kept the table clear for a week and expected recognition for it. The radiator under the window was working now, a low steady knock. The folded receipt was still in the drawer.

He had spent a week deciding what to ask, and found, sitting across from her with the late sun on the wood, that none of it would come out in order.

"Saionji-san. The week."

"Yes. I'm aware I owe you an account." She opened her notebook to a clean page and squared it. "The short version is that my grandfather has opinions. They are not new. I have dealt with them, and the matter is concluded." A pause, brief and clean. "It was a family discussion. Nobody is ill."

"That's not the question I was going to ask."

"Then ask it."

He looked at the cup between his hands. "Yui says there are three versions of where you were. One of them is a miai."

She did not answer at once. She turned her pen a quarter turn on the page, and he watched her decide whether to treat it as the question it was.

"That one has the highest rating, and it is wrong," she said. "There was no miai and there is no person. I will go to the New Year gathering because my mother asked, and I will sit through one meal, and I will leave when courtesy permits." She held his eye. "Is that satisfactory?"

"Yes."

"Good." Something in her shoulders let go by an inch. Then, in the same even voice, without any change of tone: "Has anything been different lately?"

He frowned. "Different how?"

"Anything." She pulled the pen back toward herself. "Letters. Calls. A change in your shifts. Somebody asking questions about you at the registrar."

"Why would someone be asking questions about me at the registrar?"

"I am being tedious. Ignore it."

"That isn't ignorable."

"Then humor me." She wrote a date at the top of the clean page. "Has the scholarship office contacted you since the term notice?"

"No."

"If they do, tell me."

"Why."

"Because it would be efficient for me to know." She did not look up. "Do you still take the long way home from the store?"

He set the cup down. "You're asking about my route."

"I'm asking whether you've changed it."

"No. It's the only way that isn't through the main road." He watched her, and she wrote a word he could not read. "Saionji-san. Is something going to happen?"

"I don't know of anything that is." She said it quickly and exactly, like a figure. "I'd simply like to know whether anything has."

It was an answer, and it wasn't one. He had spent two years learning to tell the difference in her voice, and he heard it now, the way you hear a floorboard that hasn't been repaired yet, taking weight.

"The roster," he said. "Your text said routine, apparently."

"I said it was apparently routine."

"You didn't say you believed it."

Reina set down the pen. For a moment she looked at him with an expression he could not classify, the look of someone who has been caught doing arithmetic aloud.

"This is inefficient," she said. "We're both interrogating each other about whether anything is different."

"Yes."

"I would like us to stop."

"All right." He picked up his own pencil. "You tell me when it's something. I'll tell you when it's something."

"That is an extremely vague agreement."

"It's what we've got."

She considered it with the seriousness she gave to a footnote. Then she nodded once, slowly, and pulled the clean page toward the center of the table, in the space between them where the notebook usually sat.

___

They worked for two hours. It was an ordinary two hours, which was a relief, with the dictionary open on a word neither of them trusted and her handwriting and his drifting across the same pages the way they had before the week began. Once, around seven, she reached across without comment and moved his glasses two centimeters up his nose, where they had slid when his eyes closed.

"You were asleep," she said.

"I was reading."

"You were reading with your eyes closed, for eleven seconds." She went back to her page. "Is the shift cutting into your sleep."

"It evens out."

"That is what you say. I would like the actual number."

"Five hours."

"Five hours is not an evening out." She wrote it down. He watched her write it in black, not red, and did not know whether that was good news.

At the door of the library, packing his bag, he counted what she had asked him in two hours: letters, calls, shifts, routes, sleep. Every question had been about the next few weeks. He noticed it, and had nowhere to put it.
