# Chapter 36: Too Clean

*(Saionji Reina, going back to the thirtieth of November)*

She did not need a map. The flashing lights were visible from the corner of the store, two blocks down, turning the frost on the school railings red and white and red again.

Reina arrived at 6:14 with her bag on her shoulder and chalk to the wrists, and stopped at the edge of the tape because a policeman was standing in front of it. Beyond him the crossing was a wide gray sheet with a dull sheen. An ambulance had been and gone; the evidence of it was a litter of torn packaging and a long dark smear she did not look at for longer than a glance. A man in an orange vest was working along the gutter with a handcart, throwing salt in a measured arc.

"The salt," she said, to nobody.

"Came late," the policeman said, as though it were a regrettable fact about the municipality. "Are you family, miss?"

"I'm his classmate."

"Then I can't say anything. He's been taken to the general hospital on Route 9." He looked at her hands. "You'll want to wash those."

She did not. She turned her head instead, because someone had begun to speak behind the policeman, and it was a voice she registered at once for how little it varied.

A dark sedan stood at the curb with its hazard lights on and a crease across the front bumper. Beside it a man in a gray overcoat, fifties, was talking to a second officer with his hands held flat at his sides. He did not pace. He did not smoke. He stood the way people stand in front of a grave. "It was my fault," she heard him say. "The whole of it. I braked too late. I will write that down for you, as many times as you need."

She noted it, and did not know yet why she had.

___

The general hospital had a front desk the color of old teeth and a clerk who had not been awake for long. Reina reached it at ten to seven and learned in the first minute that the patient was in surgery, that the patient was a student and therefore not yet reachable by any next of kin, and that the ward was family only.

"I am not asking to go upstairs," she said. "I am asking for his condition."

"Family only, miss."

"That is a policy about visiting. I did not ask to visit."

The clerk gave her the unhappy, unmoved look of someone defending a rule against an argument that was probably sound. "I'm sorry. I can't."

She signed the visitor sheet anyway, because the sheet existed, and her thumb left a white print on the line. Then she sat in a plastic chair facing the desk, with her bag between her feet, and did not move.

Yui arrived at five past seven, in a coat over pajama bottoms, with Kaito behind her at a speed that suggested he had run the whole way and was now trying to pretend otherwise. Kaito went straight to the desk and began to talk to the clerk at length and with feeling. Yui sat down in the next chair.

"Do not ask me anything," Reina said.

"I wasn't going to." Yui put a hand on her sleeve and left it there. It was warm, and Reina did not move her arm.

The minutes went by on the wall clock in jerks. At 7:40 a nurse came out and told Kaito, who had somehow become the person the building was prepared to speak to, that the surgery was proceeding and that the university's emergency contact, a Shindou Yumi, had been telephoned. At 8:05 another door opened, and the man in the gray overcoat came through it.

He had changed. That was the first thing she saw. The overcoat was gone, and under it he wore a dark suit, pressed, with a plain tie, and he carried a small paper bag in both hands. He walked to the desk, stopped a pace short of it, and bowed.

It was a good bow. It was held for a count of four and executed from the hips with the back flat, a depth she associated with funerals and with people who had practiced.

"I am the driver," he said. "I am the one who struck him. I would like to leave this for the family, and this." The bag, a card, a slip of paper on which a string of digits had been written in block capitals. "That is the claim number. I telephoned my insurer from the roadside at a quarter to seven. They have opened it already. Please send every bill to them. Whatever he needs, whatever it costs, please say that it is mine."

The clerk took the card in both hands. "Sir, you don't need to..."

"I do," the man said. "Please."

Reina watched him bow a second time, at exactly the same angle, and turn, and walk out through the automatic doors into the morning.

Beside her, Kaito sat down heavily, looking stunned and almost grateful. "That's... decent of him," he said. "That's honestly decent."

"Yes," Reina said.

She watched the doors close. Then she opened her notebook on her knee.

___

She could have taken it as decency. It was available to her, a clean conclusion, and she weighed it for exactly as long as it deserved. People who struck pedestrians were sometimes ashamed. People who were ashamed sometimes acted at once. A man who had been brought up to bow could be expected to bow.

But a man who admitted the whole of the fault before the report was written was either very honest or very well briefed. A man who had the claim number on a card in block capitals at five past eight had performed a sequence that, in her experience of people in shock, took them far longer to find. A man who went home and changed into a suit before coming to a hospital where nobody was awake enough to look at him had been told the room would be watched.

Too clean. It was an inexpert phrase, and she could not improve on it.

She turned back a page in the notebook, to a week she had not meant to revisit. It had been the week she was called home in November, in the long room at her grandfather's house, after the other guests had finished their tea and gone. Her grandfather had sat for a time turning the stem of his cup in its saucer, the way he did when he was composing something in his head, and then he had said, to the room and not to her: *If this continues, do not blame me for the measures I take.*

She had asked which part of it continued.

He had not answered. She had not been afraid. She had finished her tea.

She was not afraid now, she noted. She was, if anything, extremely clear. She uncapped the red pen she kept for matters that would be done, and wrote three lines on a fresh page, small and level, in the hand she used for proofs.

*1. Full admission before the report.*

*2. Claim number at 8:05.*

*3. "Measures."*

She read the three lines once. She did not underline them, because underlining was for conclusions, and she was a person who did not draw conclusions from fewer than four points.

Yui read the page upside down. She said nothing, which from Yui was the loudest remark in the building.

At ten to nine the clerk came out from behind the desk and told Kaito he could go up for ten minutes as the roommate, authorized by telephone. Kaito looked at Reina. Reina looked at the clock.

"Go," she said.

She stayed where she was, in the plastic chair, chalk drying to a pale crust in the lines of her hands, until it was twelve minutes past nine and the door to the stairs had closed behind him for the second time. Then she stood, shouldered her bag, and left, because there was one thing the regulations had not yet thought to forbid, and a florist on Route 9 opened at ten.
