# Chapter 35: Can I Call You Reina

He told himself he was not looking for anyone.

The bus terminal was half empty at that hour, a Tuesday, mid-morning, with the stillness of people who weren't going anywhere urgent. He stood by the vending machines with the duffel over one shoulder and a crutch under the other arm, and did not look at the entrance every ninety seconds. He looked at the departure board instead, and then at the entrance anyway, and told himself the second look was an accident.

Kaito had said goodbye at the dormitory gate, in the end, by hugging him with the crutch trapped awkwardly between them and saying into his shoulder that he would take the bus himself if Itsuki tried the monk routine over text. Itsuki had said he would not. Kaito had said he knew he was lying and loved him anyway, and gone back up the stairs very fast.

Nobody came through the terminal doors. The board changed. Twelve minutes to boarding became nine, then six. He adjusted the strap on his bag and made himself stop checking, because no version of the morning existed in which checking changed anything, and a man who had signed the end of two and a half years did not also get to stand around waiting for someone to tell him it mattered.

At three minutes he turned toward the gate.

That was when he heard it. Footsteps, too fast for a Tuesday, uneven in the way of someone not used to running, and something in his chest turned him around before the rest of him had decided to.

Reina.

She stopped a few meters off with one hand braced on her knee, breathing in ragged pulls that fogged in front of her. Her coat was buttoned one hole off. A long piece of hair had come out of its pins and hung across her face in a way he had never once seen it hang. Her glasses sat crooked, there was sweat at her temple despite the cold, and her eyes were already red, not only from the running.

She straightened. The first thing out of her was not composed in the least.

"You did not tell me."

He had nothing ready for that.

"Yui informed me at nine forty-seven. Kaito informed Yui last night. I learned it in a corridor, from a text message, like a change of room." Her voice cracked in the middle and she did not rebuild it. "I walked out of a lecture. I have never walked out of a lecture."

"I'm sorry."

"I did not come for an apology." She pressed her lips together, visibly pulling the next sentence back before it went somewhere she could not follow it. When it came, it was quieter, and worse for that. "We agreed. When it is something."

"We did."

"This is something."

"It is."

"Then why was it not said."

He looked at her, at the crooked glasses and the coat buttoned wrong, two and a half years of architecture standing in a bus terminal with none of it holding. He gave her the true answer, since it was the only one that would fit.

"Because if I'd told you before it was decided, you'd have found a route. And I'd have let you take it. And you'd have spent another four hours next to a man who talks about golf."

Her mouth opened and closed. "That was my decision."

"It's mine to stop asking you to make them."

"I had drafted three alternatives." She said it to the terminal floor, a figure read out. "The third involved money. I would have presented it last, so that you could refuse me and not Kaito."

"I would have refused."

"Naturally. I had accounted for that." A breath, uneven. "I ran from the south gate. I would like that noted."

"Noted," Itsuki said, and watched it land somewhere behind her eyes in a way that made him wish he had chosen another word.

The speaker overhead crackled and announced the highway bus at bay four, doors closing in two minutes. Neither of them moved for a second longer than the announcement allowed, as if the sound had to cross the whole distance between them before either could act on it.

"Can I call you Reina," he said.

She blinked. Whatever she had braced for, it was not that.

"Instead of Saionji-san. If it's allowed."

Something in her face came apart in a different direction than the tears had been heading. It was softer, and nearer to disbelief, and she nodded, once, with no sound, because whatever she might have said was still stuck where the rest of it had stuck.

"Reina." He tried it aloud, a name he had spent two and a half years refusing to say, and it felt absurdly like the largest thing he had done that month, larger than the form, larger than the signature. "When you have time, you could visit. Summer, maybe. You've already found the way once."

"That is not an answer to anything."

"No. It isn't."

"Summer," she repeated, testing it before she trusted it. "That is months away."

"Four, if the calendar's honest. I'll be somewhere easy to find."

"And the procedure." Her voice had gone thin. "When you arrive, you will send the word."

"I'll send it."

"You may not omit it on the grounds that it is no longer a procedure."

"I won't."

She stood there with her mouth slightly open around a sentence that would not finish. "What happens. To us. Going forward. I... I will..."

It caught in the place his own had always caught. She did not manage to finish it, and the tears came past whatever had been holding them, and he understood that this was simply how the two of them were going to do this for a while: reaching the exact edge of the thing and finding nothing waiting on the other side.

He did not push. He reached out instead. He had no precedent for this either, any more than for the name, but his thumb found the wet track beneath her eye and wiped it away, unhurried, as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world and not a gesture two and a half years in the making.

"Being able to make you cry," he said, quietly, with something like wonder under the dryness, "might be the biggest thing I've accomplished at this school."

A sound came out of her that was half a laugh and half something rawer, and she swiped at her own face with the back of her wrist as though she could still rescue some of the old order by force. "That is an appalling thing to be proud of."

"I didn't say proud. I said accomplished."

"They are the same thing, Shindou-san."

"Itsuki," he said, and watched that land too.

The doors chimed a warning. "And you," she said, quieter. "What happens to you."

"I'll manage." He said it simply, with no performance left in either of them. "It's the one thing I've always been good at. Not the exams. Working out how to keep going once the plan stops working. I'll adapt. I'll work. I've done both since I was twelve, according to my mother, who brought it up recently and didn't mean it as praise."

"That is not a plan."

"No. But it's mine. I'd rather have that than a better one that isn't."

He shifted his weight onto the crutch and glanced at the gate, and then back at her, hair loose, glasses crooked, eyes wet, the whole careful structure of her standing in a bus terminal with none of it doing its job. Leaving felt like the wrong direction, which he had never once believed about a bus. He got on anyway, because some decisions you have to walk through whatever your chest says about them.

The doors closed. Through the glass she had not moved, one hand half raised in something that was not a wave, and he watched the terminal slide away and kept watching after there was nothing left of it to see.

___

The bus took eleven hours, the last three of them in the dark, and his hometown looked smaller than it had last time, the way places do once you have spent long enough somewhere that measures itself in rankings instead of rooflines.

His mother's door was the same door it had always been, same worn paint, same number 4 hung a little crooked on its nail, and she opened it before he had finished knocking, as if she had been listening for him on the step.

She took one look at the crutch, the bag, his face, and knew before he had said a word.

"You withdrew."

It was not a question. He nodded anyway, since it seemed wrong to let a fact like that pass without being confirmed aloud, once, to the one person who had earned the right to hear it out loud.

"I'm sorry." It came out rougher than he intended. "Two and a half years, and the scholarship, and everything you..."

"Don't." Her voice was gentle and had no give in it, the voice she had used when he was small enough to insist he could carry both grocery bags. "Don't you dare apologize to me for getting hit by a car."

"There was a debt. After the scholarship lapsed. I could have waited, held the line for two terms, but the arithmetic never..."

"Itsuki." Only his name, and it stopped him more completely than an argument. Her hands were not steady when she reached up and touched his face, checking it for damage the way she used to when he came home with a scraped knee he had sworn was nothing. Her eyes were bright at the rims, and he watched her fight it, watched years of effort go into not letting it fall in front of him.

It fell anyway, a little, at the corner, and was quickly wiped and folded back into composure, the way she folded everything.

"It's all right," she said, and her voice shook only on the second word. "Coming home is fine. Do you understand me? Home is always fine."

He had no answer, and again it was not a failure to find one. He let her pull him in by the sleeve, crutch and all, into a house that had never once asked him to be anything except what he currently was.
