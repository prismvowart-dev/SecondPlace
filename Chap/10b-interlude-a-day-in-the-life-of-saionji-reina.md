# Interlude: A Day in the Life of Saionji Reina

At seven o'clock Reina went into the training room and shut the door. Her phone went face down on the shelf beside her glasses, on silent. The block did not accept interruptions, including from her own curiosity. The mirror along one wall was for checking form and nothing else.

At 7:40 her shirt was dark between the shoulder blades and the log had one item left, the one that decided whether the day counted. She stood at the center of the mat, then sat, and began in an L-sit: legs straight out in front of her, hands planted at her hips, arms locked, her whole body held so still that the only moving thing in the room was the dust in the window light.

No swing. No momentum. She shifted her weight forward by degrees until her shoulders stood over her wrists, then pushed. Her hips rose. Her legs followed, and the L-sit became a Mexican handstand in one continuous motion, with no kick and no wobble to mark where one position ended and the next began.

She did not stop there. She lowered her center of mass forward, slowly, legs reaching back behind her, until her body opened into a single straight line parallel to the floor.

Full planche.

Elbows locked. Shoulders pressed forward past her hands and held. Her breathing stayed level, in for four, out for four, as if the position cost nothing at all. Without her glasses the mirror was a soft smear, but the line from heels to skull was there, level as a ruled edge. She checked it the way she checked a proof, looking for the place where it broke, and it did not break.

She counted without moving her lips.

Thirty.

Thirty-five. Her left shoulder began to report, as it always did first, a fine tremor nobody who didn't know to look for it would have seen. She lowered her feet before it could become anything else. Her toes met the mat without a sound.

Thirty-five. It had been thirty-five since March. The target in the log was forty, and the log accepted no excuses. She rested three minutes and went again, because the log required three attempts: thirty-three, then thirty-one. She wrote all three down, sat back, rolled out her wrists, put her glasses on, and picked up her phone.

It was 7:52.

**Yui:** im at the beach

**Yui:** come

Both had arrived at 7:12. The third was timestamped 7:31.

**Yui:** ur not answering. im choosing to read that as yes

Reina typed with a thumb that was still damp.

**Reina:** Which beach.

The answer came before she had set the phone down.

**Yui:** the one 2 hrs from ur house by train. the one u ran away from home to when u were little 😏

Reina read the second sentence once and elected not to respond to it.

**Reina:** I have a schedule.

**Yui:** ur schedule has had since march to get u to a 40 sec planche and its failing. i think it can survive one day

**Yui:** 8:42 from ur station. bring the towel u never use and also sunscreen, ur skin doesnt know what sun is

Reina looked at the log lying open on the mat. 35, 33, 31. It was an accurate low blow, and she resented that Yui had the number memorized.

She thought of the schedule waiting on her desk. The calisthenics block was done and ticked. What remained were three reading blocks, a review of the joint project, and the one hour in the afternoon labeled *unstructured leisure*, which she had never once used for leisure. She told herself that a single day of structured relaxation was not a deviation from the schedule but a fulfillment of it. The hour existed for a reason. She had simply never had an occasion.

She was aware, even as she assembled this, that the argument had been built backward from its conclusion. She made it anyway. She showered in four minutes, a record she told nobody, and was on the platform at 8:36 with six minutes to spare.

___

The beach was smaller and emptier than she'd expected. A curve of grey-gold sand sat below a fishing harbor, boats stacked along a breakwater, in the kind of town that existed for the people who lived there rather than the people who visited. Yui was already near the waterline in a wide sunhat that had never served a practical purpose, waving with both arms as though Reina might otherwise fail to find her on an empty stretch of coast.

"You came," Yui said, sounding delighted, which Reina chose not to examine.

"You told yourself I was already coming. I didn't want to make you a liar."

"That's not why you came."

"That is exactly why I came."

Yui grinned the grin that meant she'd decided not to push, because she had already won, and steered Reina down the sand by the elbow, talking about the harbor bakery with a smile that stayed fixed in place a little too well. They were halfway down the beach when Reina saw him.

He was thirty meters off, crouched at the waterline in swim shorts and a plain gray t-shirt, helping a small boy retrieve a red bucket that had washed half into the surf. Shindou Itsuki, indisputably, in a context so far from anything she associated with him that her first instinct was to assume she'd misidentified a stranger. He straightened, said something that made the boy laugh, turned, and stopped when he saw her, the same way she had stopped seeing him.

Her first classification was *coincidence*. She turned to say so and found Yui pressing the back of one hand to her mouth, as if suppressing a yawn at a quarter to eleven in the morning, with the studied blankness of a woman who had known exactly what she was doing.

Reina took the blankness for what it was, which was evidence.

"You," he said, when they'd closed enough distance to make speaking necessary, in the tone of a man still auditing the situation for errors, "do not strike me as someone who goes to the beach."

"I could say the same of you."

"I grew up fifteen minutes from here. I have a functional excuse."

"I was invited. That's also a functional excuse."

He looked at Yui. "You knew."

"I wish I could take credit." Yui dropped the blank face at once and looked, if anything, thrilled. "I came around the hut ten minutes ago and he was standing there holding a bucket. I almost dropped my melon soda. I've been trying not to look at him since."

Reina searched Yui's face for rehearsal, the way she searched a bad proof for the seam, and found none. The coincidence was real. It had been sitting in the sun for ten minutes, waiting for her to arrive.

That was worse.

___

The volleyball happened mostly because Yui insisted, and partly because the two boys passing a soft, battered ball near the shoreline were people Itsuki had known since before either of them could reliably spell his own name. They greeted him with the unguarded ease of people from before Koyo, before the results board, before any of it.

"Itsuki's terrifying at school, right?" the taller one said to Reina, in the voice of a man setting up a joke he'd told before. His name was Yamazaki. "Top of the year, works himself into the ground. You'd never guess he used to cry about jellyfish."

"I was seven."

"You were eleven."

Itsuki looked displeased in the way only childhood embarrassment could produce. It wasn't anger, just the resignation of a man whose past had volunteered itself to a stranger. Reina took this as data: he could be mortified. He didn't usually show it.

"I have a photograph," Yamazaki said, with relish, and Reina made a private note to acquire it by whatever means became necessary, and declined, even to herself, to name the category she'd put it in.

They tied a length of rope between the lifeguard hut's corner post and a stake the other boy, Ren, hammered in with his shoe. Yui scratched the court into the sand with her heel, side lines wobbly, back lines dead straight, because she cared about the back lines. Then she divided the teams by pointing: Reina and herself on one side, the three boys on the other.

"Two against three?" Reina said.

"The boys' side has Shindou. It balances."

"I'm not useless," Itsuki said.

"You're a rounding error," Yui said, kindly.

Reina pulled her sundress off and stood in her swimsuit and the plain white shirt she had layered over it, and on the next stretch of sand two college-age boys stopped mid-rally. The ball dropped unattended between them. Reina gave them the look she reserved for pamphlet canvassers, and they discovered something fascinating about the horizon.

She hadn't played since middle school. She'd been good at it, in the way she was good at anything with rules and a score, and the body remembered before she did. The first ball Yui bumped toward her came in low, and she was under it before she'd decided to move. A clean two-handed set, soft arc, exactly where Yui could reach it. Yui drove it into the sand at Yamazaki's feet.

On the other side of the rope, Itsuki had gone still, one knuckle halfway to his glasses.

"You've done that before."

"Middle school. Club. It wasn't relevant."

"It feels relevant."

"It's volleyball. It isn't complicated."

She allowed herself the small private satisfaction of surprising him, gone before anyone could catch it on her face. Then Yamazaki and Ren got going.

They had been playing together since childhood, and it showed. A ball would arrive at Ren's hands and he wouldn't look where it went, because Yamazaki was already there. Itsuki was the kind of player who got better the longer he was shown something, and he took her hardest serves on flat, unhurried forearms, annoyingly on time. Whatever lead she and Yui built in the first ten minutes, the boys erased by degrees. By the time the sun had climbed high enough to make everyone squint, it was 10–10.

"Match point," Yui said. "Reina. Be nice."

"I'm always nice."

"Sure," Yui said. "I trust you completely." She said it in the voice of a woman who trusted nothing about the situation.

Reina collected the ball and walked to the baseline. Six paces. A jump serve in sand was a stupid idea. Sand ate the legs, stole the height, gave nothing back. She knew that. She had also done nine hundred squat jumps that week, and her calves had stopped negotiating with sand.

She threw the ball up.

It wasn't a serve's toss. It was a flare. The ball climbed past the lifeguard hut's roof, past the gull standing on the roof, and the gull, offended, left. It kept going. It became a small dark dot against a pale sky.

"Itsuki," Yamazaki said, on the far side of the rope, in a very quiet voice. "What is she doing."

"I don't know."

"That's not a serve. That's a launch."

"Back up," Ren said.

They backed up. Yamazaki went first, jogging, then walking backward faster than he'd have chosen, past the scratched line, past the cooler, until his shoulders met the harbor wall. Ren went the other way and let the sea reach his ankles in protest. Itsuki went back to Yui's back line and stopped. The court ended there, and he was a man who believed in the court. He stood with his toes against the line, hands set at chest height, jaw tight.

Reina ran. Three strides, plant, drive. The sand gave under her left foot and she let it, and the rise came anyway, up through the hips and shoulders. Her arm went back and came through, and the sound of the hit was a flat, hard crack, like a door slamming in a very large house.

She watched the ball cross the rope low, by a hand's width, and kept watching, because she knew what was coming. It flew flat and fast toward the back of the court. At the baseline it was at head height. For a man standing on the baseline, head height at the baseline meant long.

Itsuki did the geometry. "Out," he said, in the calm voice of someone announcing a train, and sat down in the sand to let it pass over him.

It was the correct response to a long ball. The ball was not long. The topspin took it all at once, as if it had struck a wall, and it dropped in a curve so steep that it arrived at the line at the same instant as the line's occupant, at the exact height of his seated face.

It hit him there.

The sound was a single clap, close to a microphone. His glasses left his head and landed upside down in the sand a meter behind him. He went over backward slowly, as though lying down had been his plan from the start, and the ball rolled to a stop beside his ear and waited.

___

They ended up on the sand a little above the waterline, because someone had to sit and he was the only one with a medical reason.

Reina had spread the towel she never used and he had taken one corner of it without being invited, which she allowed. His glasses were back on his face, one lens faintly salted; she had polished them on her shirt hem herself and returned them without remark. A bruise was starting along his left cheekbone, the color of a peach with ambitions.

Out on the sand, Yui, Yamazaki, and Ren had found a crab and were chasing it in loose, shrieking circles, a committee with no agenda. At one point Yui looked back at the two of them on the towel, a long, bright look, and then turned and ran after the crab with her whole body, as if that was what she had meant to do all along. Reina noted it as the behavior of someone checking on a project.

She held out a can she had bought from the vending machine behind the lifeguard hut. Barley tea, the coldest one in the machine.

"Press that against it."

He pressed it against it, and read the label. "Barley tea."

"It was the coldest."

For a minute neither of them said anything. The sea went on doing its one unremarkable thing. She watched Yui chase the crab in a circle, and heard herself ask it, with her eyes still on the water.

"Does it still hurt?"

"Less than it's going to look tomorrow."

"I'll take that as a yes."

"Take it as a mild yes."

"I wasn't aiming for you."

"I was on the line. It was at head height. It was long."

"It was long until it wasn't."

"I'm aware of that now." He shifted the can against his cheek. "You left the ground. In sand. I'd like that on the record, because I intend to remember it."

"Remember it for what."

"Future arguments. I'll be conducting them from at least ten meters."

"That's cowardice."

"That's a risk assessment."

She very nearly smiled, and looked at the water until it passed. He laughed, a short surprised sound, and winced, and put the can back where it belonged. It was a good laugh. She noted it, and put it with the others.

Her attention slid, without her permission, to his forearm resting on his knee. He was thinner than she'd accounted for. Not unhealthy, exactly, but built like someone whose meals always came second to whatever else needed doing, with wrists defined more by shift work and walking than by anything deliberate. It was a practical observation, the kind she'd have made about anyone.

"You should eat more," she said, before she'd fully decided to say it.

He looked down at himself, then at her. "That's abrupt."

"It's accurate. You work a shift that would break most people and you look like someone who forgets meals exist."

"Noted." A pause. "Convenience store wages don't leave much for nutrition."

He said it flatly, as a fact about someone else's problem, and she understood that he was showing her something. Not all of it, but enough that she couldn't pretend not to know anymore.

"Then the observation stands," she said, and left it there.

___

The day wound down the way beach days apparently did, which was information Reina had not previously possessed. The light went long and gold. Yamazaki and Ren peeled off toward home with the easy goodbyes of people who would see each other in a week without scheduling it. Yui waded out to her ankles with her hat pushed back, humming something tuneless and off-key.

"We should head back before the last train," Reina said at last, standing and brushing sand from her legs with more attention than the task required.

"Probably."

Neither of them moved right away.

On the train, sitting by the window as the coastline folded itself away behind her, Reina opened her calendar. The calisthenics block from that morning sat green and ticked. Below it, in the afternoon row she'd labeled in June, was the other one: *unstructured leisure*. It had been blank every day since she drew the grid.

She deleted the label. She typed a single word.

*Beach.*

She read it back once, found nothing in it to correct, and left it.
