# Chapter 24: The Third Board

Spring term ended the way it always did, in a flurry of citations and proofs, the library keeping its lights on later than the building was supposed to allow and Reina claiming the good chair each morning as if defending a border nobody else had the nerve to test. The board in the main hall went up twice more, and both times it said the same thing, and neither of them mentioned it.

In March she told him, in the voice she used for weather, that her mother had reviewed the arrangement and that it stood. He did not ask what that meant. He had seen her wrists go on turning under the table for months, and he knew enough by then to leave a sentence like that where she had put it.

Then the calendar opened up in front of him, six empty weeks, the way it did every year, and he filled them the way he always filled them: the night shift, his mother's kitchen, a bus route short enough to ride with his eyes half shut. They did not write to each other over the summer. Neither of them was built for it.

He came back to Koyo in the first week of September for what he had begun to think of, somewhere over the summer, as the third time rather than a new one.

The gate looked the same. The trees had traded April's brief performance for a heavier, more permanent green, and a fresh crop of first-years had stopped dead beneath them to photograph it anyway. Near the bulletin board two of them argued in good-natured circles over which club met on Tuesdays. One of them was about the kendo hall by the south gate, which he knew met at dawn, and he did not offer that either.

At the vending machines by the dormitory, the one person whose business he apparently still was stood reading her phone.

"You're early," Reina said, not looking up.

"You're earlier."

"I have a system." She put the phone away before he could see the screen. She wore a white shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, her hair down, the thin frames on her face, and a first-year buying tea beside her had just dropped his change into the slot of the machine, one coin at a time, without taking his eyes off her. "The board resets on the fifth. I intend to be first in line to read it, on the theory that dignity requires seeing the result before anyone else does."

"It'll say what it says."

"That is not comfort."

"It isn't meant to be." He looked at her wrists, because they were what he looked at. "You still do that."

"I'm aware." She turned her hands over, as though checking them. "Kuze-senpai has asked me a third time, by the way. About open practice. I have begun to consider the persistence a personality trait." She started toward the dormitory block, and he fell in beside her. "You've lost weight."

"It was a long summer."

"Four kilos, by the look of your collar. I'll bring something Thursday."

The library, when they finally sat down to it on the first real Thursday of term, had rearranged its noise the way it always did after a long quiet: first-years fumbling with the checkout machine, someone's phone buzzing three tables away before an embarrassed scramble to silence it, the radiator under the window clanking on at four, as it had for two years running. Reina had already claimed the window seat. She did it now without either of them discussing whose habit it had been.

He set two coffees down. She had stopped pretending to be surprised by the second one sometime last year, though she still never thanked him for it directly.

What she did instead, this time, was produce a container from her bag and set it between them with the briskness of someone performing a chore. "I made too much again."

"You always make too much."

"It is an inefficiency in my cooking, not a gesture." She opened her notebook, though the tips of her ears had gone faintly pink. "Take it or don't. I'm not carrying it home."

He took it. He always did. He had worked out, somewhere around the fifth container, a year ago, that commenting on the pattern would end the pattern, the way naming a stray cat guaranteed you'd have to feed it forever, except that in this case forever seemed to be the part neither of them minded.

He had brought something too. It was small enough that he had almost not done it: a paper bag of the roasted chestnuts from the stall by the station, which she had mentioned once, eleven months ago, in a sentence that had not been addressed to him and that he had not technically been supposed to hear. He put it on the table without comment, the way she put things on the table, and returned to his notes.

She looked at the bag for a long time. Long enough that he almost regretted it.

"I don't recall telling you I liked these."

"You didn't tell me."

"Then how..."

"You told Yui. Last October." He didn't look up from the page. "I was there."

She was quiet after that, and when he glanced over, something had happened to her composure that she was working, with visible effort, to put back where it had been. She didn't thank him. She opened the bag a thumb's width, breathed in, and made a small, private sound of approval that she immediately pretended had not occurred. Two aisles over, Mori's cart squeaked past the end of the row, and the bag vanished into Reina's tote with the speed of a woman concealing evidence.

"The pencil you use," she said, changing the subject with the subtlety of a woman closing a door, "is discontinued. I looked it up."

"I know. I have four left."

"I've located a supplier in Osaka." She said it to her notebook. "I'm not telling you this because I care. I'm telling you because running out in the middle of an exam would be inefficient, and inefficiency in a study partner reflects poorly on my own preparation."

He let her have the fiction. Taking it away from her had never gotten him anything he actually wanted.

Her phone buzzed on the table, face down, twice in quick succession. She did not turn it over. He saw her not turn it over, and saw, too, the way her jaw did something before she smoothed it away. It was not fear but it stood next to fear, the look of someone doing arithmetic on a number she hadn't wanted to see yet.

"Everything all right?"

"My grandfather's assistant. Scheduling for the holidays. Nothing urgent." She said it in the flat, complete cadence she used for facts she had already decided not to discuss, and he recognized the cadence, and let it be.

The radiator clanked again, off schedule this time. Outside the window the evening had gone the grey-blue it went in early September, not yet autumn. Neither of them said the words *third year* out loud, though both of them were aware, each in a way the other couldn't see, of exactly how many were left.
