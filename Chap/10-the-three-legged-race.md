# Chapter 10: The Three-Legged Race

Kaito found him after his shift the next morning, which meant he'd been waiting, which never boded well.

"Before you see the sheet," he said, "I need you to know I had a plan, the plan changed, and I would like partial credit for telling you before a bulletin board did it for me."

"What did you do."

"I un-signed you. This morning, before anyone else got to the gym. And signed myself with Tachibana instead." Both hands went up before Itsuki could say anything. "She asked. I want that on the permanent record. She asked me."

"You told me you'd signed us up together on Tuesday."

"That was also true. At the time."

Itsuki didn't say anything else about it. There wasn't a version of the sentence that would help, and he'd learned by now that arguing with Kaito's timeline of events cost more than it returned.

"So I don't have a partner."

"You have several options. None of them involve me, is the part I need you to accept quickly."

He got the rest of the story from Reina herself, an hour later, outside the seminar room, before he'd even set his bag down.

"Tachibana withdrew from our event," she said, in the flat, administrative tone she reserved for reporting a casualty rather than a betrayal. "She informed me this morning. I'm quoting her directly: an opportunity had presented itself that she felt unable to pass up."

"That's Kaito."

"I'm aware."

"He un-signed my name to put down hers. For his own event."

Something crossed her expression, quick, the look of someone running the same arithmetic he'd already run and arriving at the same inconvenient sum. "So we are both, independently, without partners, for an event that is, I'm told, mandatory for seminar students. No exceptions."

"It appears so."

She tapped one finger against the corridor wall, in the rhythm he'd learned to recognize as her actually thinking rather than her merely being annoyed.

"It would be far more efficient," she said, "than either of us being assigned a stranger by the organizing committee."

"It would."

"I want it understood that I am not suggesting this because I have run out of other options."

"I didn't say you were."

"Good." She opened the small notebook she carried for exactly this kind of decision and closed it again just as fast, the gesture she used to end a conversation to her own satisfaction. "We'll need to practice. I refuse to fall over in front of the entire cohort, Shindou-san."

___

They met behind the gymnasium on a Thursday evening, once the courts had emptied out and the only light left was the kind that made everything look slightly more dramatic than it actually was. A length of cloth from the equipment room went between his right ankle and her left one, tied by Reina herself, twice, with the same precision she brought to footnotes.

They fell over twice in the first two minutes.

"Inside leg," she said, for the third time, gripping his shoulder hard enough that he'd have a mark there tomorrow if he was being honest about it. "You keep leading with the wrong one."

"I'm leading with the one that feels correct."

"Your feeling is incorrect. This isn't a matter of feeling."

"It's very much a matter of feeling. That's what a leg is."

She didn't have anything clever ready for that, which he counted, privately, as a small personal victory.

They fell a third time, and this time he caught most of her weight going down, some reflex he hadn't consciously authorized, and for a stretch they simply didn't move. Her hand was fisted in the sleeve of his shirt. Her shoulder was braced under his arm, and even tangled and off-balance, she'd caught herself with the same quick, automatic control he'd noticed once before, at the library, when she'd rolled her shoulders back between one page and the next like her body ran on a schedule her studying didn't know about. It wasn't the balance of someone whose exercise was the walk to class. Her hair had come half out of its clip on the way down, one loose length of it falling across her collarbone, and she was breathing harder than the fall itself should have accounted for, though it settled faster than his did.

"This is humiliating," she said, not moving.

"A little."

"I have never once fallen over in public in my entire life."

"You've fallen over three times in the last six minutes."

"That is a private courtyard behind a gymnasium at seven in the evening. It does not count as public."

He let that stand, mostly because arguing it would have required moving, and for a few seconds nobody seemed motivated to do that either.

They found the rhythm by the sixth attempt, more or less: an ungainly, shuffling gait that wasn't going to win anything but wasn't going to end with either of them on the ground either.

___

Festival day arrived with the chaos of an entire university deciding, once a year, to stop pretending it took itself seriously. The main field had been sectioned into a dozen competing events, banners strung between poles that had clearly not been checked for structural integrity since the previous year, and Kaito had appointed himself team captain of a relay he wasn't even competing in.

"Shindou! Saionji! You're up in ten!" someone from the organizing committee shouted, consulting a clipboard with the harried energy of a person who had been shouting names all morning and had stopped caring how any of them sounded.

The race went, in the end, about as well as their sixth practice attempt had: not gracefully, but upright. They came in fourth out of seven pairs, beaten decisively by two second-years who had clearly been training since spring, and crossed the finish line still tangled together, both of them breathing hard, half the cohort cheering for the winners and the other half already losing interest and drifting toward the food stalls.

"Fourth," she said, straightening up, glasses faintly crooked, a flush high on her cheeks that hadn't fully settled yet. "That's acceptable."

"You said acceptable margins of failure. Twice."

"I was managing expectations."

"Whose."

She didn't answer that, and he let it go, the same way he was learning to let go of most things she chose not to answer, on the theory that pushing had never once gotten him anywhere with her that waiting hadn't gotten him faster.

___

The rest of the afternoon dissolved into the chaos festivals were built for. Kaito nearly got disqualified from his relay for a false start he swore was someone else's fault. Reina got cornered by two underclassmen who wanted to know, with real and slightly panicked interest, how she'd placed fourth instead of first, as though the concept were personally distressing to them.

Itsuki drifted through most of it the way he drifted through most things: present, mildly amused, not quite the center of anything. Waiting in line for water sometime in the late afternoon, with nothing to occupy him, he found himself scanning the crowd without having decided to, and realized, a beat too late, that he had been looking for her before he'd registered looking for anyone at all.

He found her easily enough, still arguing with the underclassmen about the definition of acceptable, Yui standing beside her failing to hide her amusement.

He looked away before he'd worked out what to do with the observation, and went back to waiting for water, telling himself it hadn't meant anything.

It was, he was fairly sure, just a crowded field. A person looks for a familiar face in a crowded field. That was all it was.

He almost believed it.
