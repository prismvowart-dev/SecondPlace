# Chapter 32: The Board Doesn't Say Why

The thirtieth of December came and went on a Wednesday, and nobody mentioned it.

Itsuki had moved to the rehab ward by then, one floor down and far more cheerful, the air smelling of liniment and the corridor television permanently tuned to a quiz show nobody was watching. Kaito arrived with mandarins. His mother phoned at four. The physiotherapist, a brisk woman named Tsuda who treated his tibia as a personal project, had him upright between two rails for the length of a song on the nurses' radio, and declared it "not a disgrace." At five o'clock the deadline passed, six kilometers away, in a building that had been dark since the twenty-seventh. Nobody mentioned that either.

He had done the arithmetic on the first day. It had not changed.

___

Kaito came back from the board on the fourth of January wearing the face of a man who had rehearsed how much of this he would actually say.

"Board's up," he said, dropping onto the end of the bed without being invited. "Saionji's first. No surprise, given..." He caught the sentence before it finished, which for Kaito amounted to a minor feat.

"Given I wasn't in the room."

"Given that, yeah."

Itsuki did not ask the next question. He knew the shape of the answer. A name in the top place, and under it a gap where his had been for two and a half years, conspicuous the way a missing tooth is, not because of what stands there but because everyone who looks knows what doesn't.

"People noticed," Kaito said, since Itsuki hadn't told him not to. "There's been a name on that board every term since first year. When it isn't, people notice."

"What are they saying."

"Does it matter?"

"No. You'll tell me anyway."

Kaito had the grace to look caught. "Some think you transferred. A second-year is very committed to a theory that a research institute abroad recruited you. I did not start it, but I didn't correct it hard, because it's a better story than the real one. Somebody says you got put on probation. Somebody says there was a fight. And there's a version where you, quote, 'couldn't keep up anymore.'" His jaw set around that one. "That one I corrected. Loudly. In the cafeteria."

"You didn't need to."

"A guy was explaining to a table of first-years that Shindou 'burned out,' like you were a firework. I wasn't going to eat my lunch through that."

His phone buzzed against his knee. Then again, then a third time. He glanced at it, thumbed the screen dark, and set it face down on the blanket like a man hiding a mess rather than a message.

"Year group chat?"

"It's been going since this morning. Someone screenshotted the board with the gap in it. Forty, fifty messages. There's a poll." He winced. "I didn't vote in the poll. I turned your notifications off two weeks ago, so don't go looking."

Itsuki looked at the ceiling dashes. For two and a half years Koyo had talked about him only in the narrow vocabulary of a board and a number, which had suited him, since a number asked nothing of him. It had never required him to be interesting. This was other people building a version of his life to fill a gap he had not chosen, and voting on which version they liked, and he found it bothered him more than any of the pin or the ribs had.

"I don't want to know the rest."

"That's wise. There's a lot of them." Kaito's voice lost some of its noise. "Nobody has the real one, for what it's worth. A car accident isn't a story anybody thinks is interesting enough to invent."

"Good. I'd rather it didn't happen to me twice, once as an accident and once as a rumor."

Kaito did not push, which over the last month had become the single most reliable thing about him. He stood and stretched in the exaggerated way that meant a change of subject was coming. "Anyway. Saionji's first. A second-year called Fujimori is second, which is apparently a tragedy for Fujimori, who studied for a month for a place she got by default." He found his jacket. "Also, Saionji read the board for eleven seconds, said something to nobody, and walked straight to the exam office. Yui says there's paperwork. I'd brace."

___

She arrived at six, on the minute, in the long coat, a folder under one arm.

"I am first," Reina said, in the voice she used to open a finding.

"Kaito told me."

"I intend to decline it."

"You can't decline a rank."

"I can decline to hold one I did not contest." She did not sit. She stood at the foot of the bed with the folder against her chest, and he saw that the strand of hair that never stayed pinned was pinned today, with enormous firmness. "I submitted a request this morning that you sit the papers here, supervised, on the original schedule. It was refused at eleven. The stated ground was that absence is not an exemption." She said the last sentence with a cold satisfaction, as if she had admired its construction in spite of herself. "They are very pleased with it."

"Saionji-san."

"A rank held in someone's absence is not a rank, Shindou-san. It is a vacancy with a name on it." Her fingertip touched the bridge of her glasses, slowly, and came away. "I have therefore made other arrangements."

"What arrangements."

She opened the folder and took out a single sheet, typed, two paragraphs, with a heading and numbered clauses. She held it out at arm's length, like a notice.

"First place is held in escrow," she said. "On your return, it reverts."

He read the first clause. He read the second. There was, in the third, a provision for interest.

"You can't hold a rank in escrow."

"I have drafted terms."

He laughed. It was a mistake; the ribs reported it at once and in detail. He folded forward over the blanket with the sheet in his hand, and Reina watched him do it with her arms folded and the face of a woman whose warning had been fully vindicated.

"I did say," she said.

"You said not to laugh. You didn't say there'd be interest."

"Clause three. You may dispute it in writing."

He was still breathing carefully, one hand against his side, when he noticed that she had sat down. She had pulled the molded chair a few centimeters nearer the bed than the last time, and she was looking at the sheet in his hand rather than at him, which he had learned was how she looked at him when she meant it.

"The thirtieth was on Wednesday," she said, quietly. "I am informing you that I have not stopped."

"I know."

"I would like you to know it anyway."

He folded the sheet in half, then in half again, and put it in the drawer of the bedside table with the phone charger and the mandarin peel and the book Yamamoto had mailed on the tenth, which he had not opened.
