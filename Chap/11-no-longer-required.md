# Chapter 11: No Longer Required

Itsuki came back to Koyo in the first week of September carrying the same duffel bag he'd left with, and found the avenue under a fresh crop of first-years who had stopped dead in the middle of foot traffic to photograph the trees. The blossoms were long gone. The trees had traded April's performance for a heavy, workmanlike green, and the first-years photographed that anyway. He walked around them.

The dormitory smelled of floor polish and somebody's instant ramen. Kaito had been back since Sunday, which Itsuki could tell from the state of the room before he could tell it from Kaito. One half was bare and orderly. The other looked as if a lost-and-found had been dropped on it from a height.

"You're late," Kaito said, from his bed, without looking up from his phone. "I'd like it noted that I have been extremely disciplined for two months, and I would like to be paid for that now."

"Paid for what."

"For not bringing it up. In July. In August. Over text, when you answered 'fine' to seven separate questions about your summer." He turned the phone around. "Tachibana sent me this."

Itsuki set down his bag and looked.

The photograph had been taken from some distance and slightly above, from a harbor wall, by someone who hadn't been aiming at art. In the middle of it a young man lay flat on his back in the sand with his arms thrown wide, as if he'd been dropped there. A pair of glasses sat upside down a meter behind his head. A volleyball rested beside his ear like a second, smaller head waiting for its turn to speak. And in the near corner, blurred and mid-stride, one hand out, her own glasses crooked on her nose, was Reina, crossing a rope on her way to him with an expression he had not seen at the time. At the time he had been looking at the sky.

"A guy named Yamazaki took it," Kaito said. "He sent it to Tachibana, she sent it to me with eleven laughing faces and the caption 'scene of the crime.' I've had it since July. Do you have any idea what it cost me to wait until you walked through that door?"

"Yamazaki's an old friend. He's been waiting since I was eleven to point a camera at me."

"Well, he's used it." Kaito pulled the phone back and enlarged the corner, hungrily. "She jumped. Tachibana says Saionji jumped. In sand. And the ball came, quote, from orbit."

"It was about forty degrees."

"Nobody told me she could do that."

"Nobody told me either."

"I wasn't *there*." Kaito dropped the phone onto his chest and addressed the ceiling. "Nobody invited me. The one summer I stay home, the girl who doesn't go outdoors launches a ball into your face, and I get it as a *jpeg*."

"I wasn't invited either. I was there by accident."

"That's worse. Everything you do by accident ends up being arranged." Kaito rolled onto his side to study him. "So what happens now? Do you need to wear a helmet to the library? Do I need to start a fund?"

"I've set a policy."

"What policy."

"Ten meters. In any future disagreement."

Kaito made a sound like a kettle left on a stove and had to put his face in the pillow. Itsuki unpacked in the meantime, two shirts, the secondhand textbooks, the folded paper with his mother's handwriting on it that he set under the desk lamp without looking at it twice.

"Send me that," he said, when Kaito came up for air.

"The photo?"

"For evidence."

"Of what."

"The ball."

Kaito looked at him for a very long time with an expression of slowly dawning joy, and did not say any of the several things he clearly wanted to say, which Itsuki counted as the single most generous act of the summer. The phone in his pocket buzzed a moment later. He didn't take it out until Kaito had turned to his own bag.

___

He saw her before he reached the library, crossing the quad twenty meters ahead of him with a stack of folders against one hip.

She wore a thin lavender cardigan over a white blouse, sleeves pushed to the elbow against the last of the heat, the dusty pink of her hair tied back low with a plain black clip. Nobody had asked the quad to reorganize itself. It did anyway. Two third-years stopped mid-argument. A first-year with a camera lowered it as if she'd walked into frame without a permit, and the cluster by the bicycle racks parted around her path like water around a post. Reina walked through the gap without looking at it, as she'd walked through that gap since April.

He gave her a three-minute head start, on the theory that walking in together would look like something, and went up.

The third floor smelled the way it always did, of paper and floor wax and the green-shaded lamps warming up. Mori was at the front desk with the overnight returns, and nodded once as he passed, the nod of a woman who had never needed to ask which table was theirs. The radiator under the window was silent for the season. It was a small mercy he noticed and forgot.

Reina was already there. Four folders in four colors, the steel ruler on the right, a ministry report with a fringe of sticky tabs open in front of her, and, under it, a second book whose spine he couldn't read.

"You're late," she said, without looking up.

"For what."

She turned a page. "I don't know. It's been so long since there was a reason to be on time that I've lost the unit."

The project that had justified two people sharing one table had been submitted in June, graded in July, and closed. There was no assignment. There was no deadline, no email from Yamamoto, nothing in either of their calendars that explained why a Tuesday in September should find them across from each other with the late sun on the wood. He sat down in the chair that had become his, and set one of the two coffees he'd bought on the way beside her ruler.

She moved her stack of papers six centimeters to the left. That was thanks.

The dictionary was in the drawer, where it had lived since the second week of spring, and next to it a mechanical pencil in the off-brand hardness she pretended not to prefer. There was a fresh one beside the old one. He didn't ask.

___

"Tell me why this is wrong," Reina said, and slid the ministry report across.

It was a summary of the local allocation tax, the formula by which the national government decided how much money each town got to keep running. She had underlined a column of figures in red and written *?!* in the margin, which he had learned was the nearest she came to swearing in a library.

He read the page. "Which part am I meant to object to?"

"The basic fiscal demand calculation. It's built on population. A village loses forty people a year, its demand figure falls, its grant falls, and the road to the clinic costs exactly what it cost last year." Her pen tapped the red column. "The formula punishes towns for shrinking. It is, in effect, a machine that rewards the places that don't need it."

"It isn't punishing them. It's measuring them." He pushed his glasses up the bridge of his nose with one knuckle. "The problem is the base year. They picked a year before the last big population drop, so everything after it looks like an error to be corrected, not a trend to be funded. Change the year and the same formula says something different."

"That's what a punishment looks like. It just has a good alibi."

"It's a choice. Choices can be reversed. You can't reverse arithmetic."

"You're about to disagree with me," Reina said.

"I just did."

"No. You're about to do it harder. You did the thing with your glasses."

His hand stopped halfway back to his lap. "That isn't a thing."

"It's a thing. One knuckle, the bridge of the nose, immediately before a contradiction. I have twelve data points."

"You have twelve."

"Thirteen, as of the last sentence." She said it to the page, with the composure of a woman reporting a rainfall figure, and he noticed that the tips of her ears had gone faintly pink and that she was holding the pen with unnecessary force.

He rolled his chair back about forty centimeters.

"What are you doing."

"Distance."

"From what."

"Future disagreements. Ten meters is ideal. The library has about three."

"I have no intention of hurling anything at you in a reading room."

"You said the same about the beach."

"That was a sport. This is a footnote."

"I've seen what you do to footnotes."

For a moment her mouth did something he'd have called a smile if it had lasted. It didn't. She pulled the ministry report back toward her and made a mark in the margin, and he understood from the shape of it that she'd conceded the base year and intended to say nothing about it. He rolled the chair forward again, to a respectful thirty centimeters short of where it started.

___

She produced the bundle when the light had gone amber. It was wrapped in plain navy cloth, knotted in two loops of identical length, and she set it at his elbow with the same motion she used to lay down the ruler, as if it were a piece of stationery.

"What is this."

"Lunch. Eventually." She was reading again, or arranging her face to look like reading. "I train in the mornings. I cook in batches for it. The recipe yields six portions and I require five."

"That's a lot of arithmetic for rice."

"You said 'noted.'" She turned a page she had finished a minute ago. "On the beach. About eating more. 'Noted' is not an answer, Shindou-san. It's a placeholder. This is me collecting on the placeholder."

He looked at the knot. Under his fingers the bundle was cool and slightly heavy, something with edges inside. "I can't take this."

"It will otherwise be thrown away, and I don't carry waste home." The pen began moving again. "The chopsticks aren't disposable. Return them with the box, washed."

The library didn't permit food at the tables, and neither of them had ever tested the rule twice. He slid the bundle into the outer pocket of his bag, where it sat against his hip, faintly warm now from the sun on the window, and said, "Thank you."

"Don't make it strange."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were composing something."

He had been. He let it go.

Yamamoto found them a quarter of an hour later, which was either coincidence or wasn't, depending on how much credit anyone extended a man who had spent a full year seating the same two students next to each other. He had papers under one arm in a stack that leaned. He looked at the second coffee cup, at the ruler, at the cloth knot showing at the mouth of Itsuki's bag, and said nothing about any of it, which was in its way louder than saying something.

"Placement exam is on the twentieth," he said. "Results go up the following week. Research pairs for the autumn seminar will be assigned by standing, top two first." He adjusted his glasses. "I mention it only so you can keep your Tuesdays and Thursdays clear."

"There's no project," Reina said. "We are seated at the same table."

"So are two people on a seesaw." He was already walking away. "I'd say that's more of a collaboration than most."

She waited until he was gone. "He does that on purpose."

"Almost certainly."

"It's transparent."

"Extremely."

She put her pen down very gently, the way she did when she was angry at someone who wasn't there. "Don't say anything else," she said, and he didn't, and they stayed until the lamps came on.

___

The mailroom in the basement of the dormitory was a wall of numbered pigeonholes and a clerk who had never once looked up. His was a thin grey-blue envelope with the university crest, which meant it was from the scholarship office, which meant he read it standing with his bag still on his shoulder.

*AUTUMN TERM REVIEW: NOTICE OF REQUIREMENTS.* Spring standing, confirmed. Continuation for the autumn term was subject to two conditions, and the first was the one he already knew: standing within the top percentile of the cohort at term's end. The second was new.

*(2) For students enrolled in a faculty research seminar, demonstrated research output, as assessed by faculty evaluation.*

He read the second line twice. The first condition he could produce alone, in the gaps, on four hours of sleep, in front of nobody. The second was something a person made in front of other people, on a schedule other people set. Five hundred and eighty thousand yen a term rested on whether a stranger on a committee thought he had produced enough.

He folded the letter along its existing creases and went to work.

___

On his break he microwaved the contents in the staff room, because there wasn't any version of him that could carry a cold meal past a warm microwave. Unwrapped, the navy cloth was a square napkin, ironed. Inside was a lacquered box and, in a plain black case of its own, a pair of chopsticks that had clearly been bought for the purpose.

Grilled mackerel, salted just enough. Rice with black sesame pressed down in a neat rectangle. Simmered pumpkin, spinach in a sesame dressing, and a slice of omelet that had been folded and cut with a ruler. Everything in it had its own compartment. Nothing touched.

He ate all of it. It was, by some distance, the best thing he'd eaten since June, and he finished it faster than he'd have admitted to anyone, standing, with his back against the staff room's cheap steel shelving and the fluorescent light buzzing over his head.

Then he washed the box in the back sink and dried it with paper towels, and set it beside his bag so he wouldn't forget to return it. He put the letter in the bag, behind the textbooks, so he wouldn't have to look at it. This seemed the correct order of priorities for the hour. He went back out front and started on the rice-ball shelf.
