# Chapter 16: Marginalia

The library that afternoon had its usual stragglers: a knot of first-years cramming for a quiz, somebody's forgotten phone buzzing itself in slow circles near the returns bin, Mori working her cart of overnight returns along the stacks and nodding at them as she passed. Thursday had come, and with it the twenty minutes Reina had promised to someone who wasn't him.

She packed early, which was how he noticed. She never left the library before he did. It was an unspoken rule of the table that had held since spring. The last thing she slid into her bag was the notebook, thick now, spine gone soft from being opened flat, corners rounded by a term of being shoved into the same overstuffed bag. He watched her zip it away and thought that roughly a third of what was in there was his fault.

___

It had started in October, with the fourth container.

She had been pouring tea from her thermos and he had been staring at her notes upside down, and the guilt that had been sitting in him for weeks, quiet and uninvited, finally found somewhere to go.

"You're missing something in this section."

She looked up, unimpressed. "I am not missing anything. That derivation is correct."

"It's correct. It's not how I'd think about it."

"I wasn't aware I needed your permission for how I think about things."

"You don't." He hesitated, which he rarely did, and she noticed him doing it, which was worse than if he'd said it outright. "I've eaten four of your lunches. I don't have anything to give back except this."

She went still. "You want to write in my notebook."

"In pencil. So you can erase it if it's wrong, or if you don't want it there."

"And what would you write."

"How I'd solve it. Not instead of your way. Next to it."

She looked at him the way she'd looked at him across this same table in the spring, when he was still a puzzle she hadn't decided whether she liked being confused by. Then she slid the notebook across, open to the page, a silent invitation dressed up as indifference.

"Keep it legible. I'm not solving a second puzzle to read the first one's answer."

"I'll keep it legible."

"And don't write anything you wouldn't want a professor to see. This is an academic document, not a diary."

He wrote three lines in the margin: a shortcut through the same derivation, faster and uglier than hers, the sort of thing a life of doing everything the hard way had taught him. She read it. She said nothing. She didn't erase it.

That was the whole negotiation. Nobody called it anything, and it kept happening anyway.

By November the margins weren't enough. He started on the backs of pages, then the inside covers, then once, after a lecture that had left them irritated at the professor for the same reason and at each other for different ones, an entire page of his own reasoning laid beside hers, with a small blunt note at the bottom: *your way is faster if you already know the answer. mine's faster if you don't.*

She hadn't erased that one either. Under it, in her upright hand, she had written: *Noted. Disputed.*

Opening the notebook now meant opening two systems of thought that had learned to share a page without fighting for all of it. His writing was smaller, more slanted, prone to abbreviation. Hers was upright and spaced and dated, as though she expected the notebook to be reviewed by a committee someday. Between them lay a term's worth of the same material, argued out twice, in two hands, on every page that mattered.

He had never called it anything either. It needed a pencil, and enough margin left to write in.

___

Now she slung the bag over her shoulder.

"Twenty minutes," she said, mostly to herself, checking the time.

"You've said that already."

"I'm reminding myself. He'll want more if I let him." She touched the bridge of her glasses with two fingers, the gesture she made when she was steadying something rather than seeing it. "The café by the west gate."

"I know where it is."

"I wasn't asking you to come."

"I know that too."

She looked at him a beat too long to be nothing, and left. The library door swung shut behind her with its usual complaint about the hinge. Itsuki sat at the empty half of the table with his own book open and did not read a word of it.

___

The café by the west gate was quiet after four, which was the only reason she had suggested it. She arrived eleven minutes early, as she arrived everywhere, and found him already seated, which she logged as a point in his favor. She had looked him up the night before: Kuze Shun, third-year, politics track, captain of the kendo club, two fellowship offers. She had read the entry the way she read any directory entry, and felt nothing about any line of it. The barista, refilling the grinder, lifted two fingers without looking up. In the corner two graduate students argued about a spreadsheet, quietly and with feeling.

Kuze stood briefly as she crossed to him. Two teas waited, hers correct on the first try, which she logged as competence and set aside.

She sat where she could see the door. It wasn't a decision. It was the seat she would have taken in any room.

"I appreciate this," he said. "I know it's an odd thing to ask of a second-year."

"You're not asking me to write your thesis. You're asking about a case I found. There's a difference."

"Fair." He pulled a legal pad toward himself, and for a while it was exactly what it had been billed as. Precedent, funding formulas, a ruling a decade old that mapped onto both their arguments. He was sharp. Sharper than the directory had suggested.

He listened with his whole attention, and with something behind it that she noticed and did not trouble to identify. A person could want a great many things from a twenty-minute conversation. She had a case to explain.

She opened her notebook to the page with the citation, and he stopped mid-sentence, looking at the paper rather than at her.

"That's not your handwriting."

"No."

"There's a lot of it." He turned the notebook slightly, not presumptuously, only enough to see three more pages of the same second hand, cramped and slanted, arguing with her margins in pencil that had been erased and rewritten more than once. "Whose is it?"

She weighed it for exactly as long as it took to decide that the honest answer was also the safe one. "The top student's. In our year."

"Shindou." It wasn't a question. "You two study together, then."

"We're research partners this term."

"That's not what I asked."

She looked down at the page, his handwriting and hers tangled together across a proof neither of them had needed the other to solve, and the usual mechanism produced nothing. No diagnosis stood ready. No tidy word waited to end the thought and let her move on. There was a page, and a question, and a silence a little longer than silences are supposed to be.

"We worked together for a while," she said. "It's efficient. Two ways of solving the same problem, side by side. I've found it useful."

"Useful," Kuze repeated, mildly, and his face, until then easy to read, went blank.

"Yes."

He nodded slowly, and something moved behind his eyes, a man doing arithmetic he hadn't expected to need. Then it smoothed. "Sorry. That wasn't my business," he said, a beat too quickly, like someone who knew he had gone half a step further than the conversation had invited and was already walking it back.

"It's fine."

"It's only that people say you don't let anyone near your work. Your methods, your notes. I'd heard that about you before I ever had a class with you." He nodded at the notebook, still open between them. "That's clearly not true."

It was true. That was the part she had no answer for, not his observation but the fact that she had never noticed it was worth observing. Years of declining study partners. Redoing group assignments alone rather than splitting the credit. A reputation she had built without trying, and had never once thought to question, because it had never felt like a choice. It had felt like her.

And then a notebook that had, without her deciding it at any moment she could point to, stopped being only hers.

She closed it. Not sharply, not enough to be a statement. Just enough to end the conversation before it required her to have one. She reached for her tea.

"You wanted to discuss the funding formula," she said. "Let's discuss the funding formula."

He let her change the subject, gracefully, the way he seemed to do most things. He did not mention the notebook again. But he watched her hands for a moment as she lifted the cup, as if checking for something he hadn't asked about out loud.

Outside, the light was going grey at the edges. She thought of a table by a window, and a man at it not reading, and drank her tea.
