# Chapter 23: Open Practice

On the Tuesday after the plaque and the apology, he took the long way home, the one that passed the south gate, and this morning, for once, there was somebody on the porch of the kendo hall.

It stood beside the gate, a low wooden building with a gray tile roof and sliding doors the color of strong tea, the sort of place a person walked past a hundred times without registering it was a building at all. At ten past six it was still dark in the window glass, and from somewhere inside came the dull, regular knock of bamboo on bamboo. On the porch a man in a dark practice jacket and wide hakama was sweeping leaves off the boards with a stiff broom, slowly and with great concentration, as though he had been assigned the leaves personally.

Itsuki would have gone by. The man looked up.

"Shindou."

Kuze leaned the broom against a post. Without the jacket and the easy smile of the mixer, he looked younger, and more tired, and more like the boy in the glass cabinet, if only because he held himself the same way: straight, weight even, a man who did not mind being watched.

"You're up early," Kuze said.

"I'm coming home."

"From where?"

"Work."

Kuze nodded as if that was a full sentence, which for most people it wouldn't have been. "I wanted to congratulate you properly. On the Harrington. I did it badly at the plaque. I stood there and said nothing, which isn't the same."

"You nodded."

"That's what I mean." The corner of his mouth moved. He picked up the broom, put it down again. "May I ask you something that isn't any of my business?"

"You can ask."

"Has Saionji ever mentioned the club?"

"No."

"She studied kendo for a month, a long time ago. I've asked her to come to an open practice twice, and she's told me no, politely, both times. I thought..."

"No."

Kuze stopped. "I haven't finished the sentence."

"You were going to ask me to ask her."

There was a pause, and then the smile came back, slower than before and more honest. "Yes."

"I won't."

"May I ask why?"

"If she says no, she means no." Itsuki shifted the strap of his bag on his shoulder. He was very tired, and it made the words come out flatter than he'd have chosen. "You can ask her again, if you want. Don't ask me to ask for you."

For a moment Kuze said nothing. Behind him, inside the hall, a voice shouted a count and a dozen pairs of feet came down together on the floor. Kuze did not turn toward it.

"Fair," he said. "Thank you for not pretending." He looked at Itsuki a moment longer than the word needed, and then at the hall. "I'd like to see whether she remembers it. That's all. If she picked up a sword."

"I'll tell her you said so."

"Please do." He lifted the broom again. "Get some sleep, Shindou."

Itsuki went on down the gravel toward the dormitory. Behind him the sweeping resumed, slow and careful, and under it the knock of bamboo on bamboo, which did not stop.

He told her that evening, at the table, in the flattest way he could manage, because any other way would have made it into something.

"Kuze asked me to ask you to come to open practice at the kendo hall."

Reina did not look up from her page. "He has asked me directly, twice. I find it unusual that he's now gone around."

"I told him no."

"To the asking?"

"To the asking for him."

She put her pen down. "Thank you."

"He said you studied it. For a month."

"I did. At twelve." She straightened a sheet that did not need straightening. "Volleyball interested me more. I don't remember much of it. There's a smell, from the floor, a kind of wax. I remember the smell." A pause. "I told him I'm not training this term."

"I noticed."

"You noticed what."

"You stopped rolling your wrists." He had not planned on saying it, and he said it to the table, since the table was the least likely to react. "In November. You do it when you're thinking. For six weeks you didn't. Then last week you started again."

Reina was quiet for long enough that he checked her face, and found that she was looking at him with an expression he could not classify, something between the look she gave a flawed citation and the look she gave the dictionary when it proved her wrong.

"That's very observant," she said.

"It's a habit. I didn't mean anything by it."

"No. It's accurate." She put her hands flat on the table. They were still. "I stopped morning training in November. There is an arrangement with my family. It has conditions, and one of them is that certain things stop until my standing is corrected."

"Your standing is second. To me."

"Yes."

"Then I'm the reason."

"You are the reason I'm second." She said it without heat, like a figure on a ledger. "You are not the reason I agreed to the condition. That is mine. Please do not confuse the two. I would find it very tedious."

He had no answer, and the quiet between them was the kind that wasn't empty.

"It's a long story," Reina said, finally. "I'd rather you had it accurately than assemble it wrong out of politeness."

"I wasn't going to assemble anything."

"You would have. Quietly, the way you do everything. I'd rather save you the trouble."

She told it to the window, in the careful, recitative voice of someone repeating a fact so many times that it had stopped being information and started being weather.

"My grandfather held first place at Koyo for four consecutive years. My mother held it for three. There's a photograph in our house of the results board, though it looked different then, with both their names circled in red ink, gone brown now. It hangs in the hallway. I grew up understanding it as an instruction, rather than a keepsake."

"That's a great deal to hand a child."

"It wasn't handed. It was simply the shape of the house." She looked at him then, directly, and the guard she usually kept behind at least one more door had come down for a moment. "I'm telling you because you already know most of it in pieces, and I would rather you had the photograph than the rumor."

He didn't have a response ready. He didn't try to invent one.

He thought of his own mother's voice on the phone at four in the morning, her knees, the fare she'd put aside, the sums he did on his breaks and had never told anyone. Reina had just handed him a piece of herself heavier than anything he had offered back. He did not offer it now either.

He noticed himself not offering it. It was the first time the noticing had actually bothered him.
