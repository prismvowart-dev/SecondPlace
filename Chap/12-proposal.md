# Chapter 12: Proposal

The placement exam ran three hours, and afterward nobody discussed it. People drifted out of the hall with the glazed look of the freshly graded, and the few who did talk about it at the vending machines did so in the low voices of people confessing.

Reina was already at the machines when he got there, which he had learned not to read as coincidence. She bought a canned tea she didn't want. He bought nothing, as usual.

"How was it," he said, because somebody had to.

"Adequate. The third question was poorly worded."

"You got it wrong."

"I did not say that."

"You only call a question poorly worded when you got it wrong."

"That is an unfounded pattern you've invented."

"I have four examples."

She turned the can a quarter turn in her hand, and he watched her decide whether to ask which four. "I would prefer that you didn't list them," she said, in the voice of a woman who knew exactly which four.

He didn't list them. He noticed that he'd started keeping a count of his own, and that he hadn't announced it either.

___

The results went up the Friday of the following week, and he missed the posting because he was asleep. He had clocked out at five, slept until ten, and made his noon lecture on the principle that attendance was a form of waking. By the time he came through the main hall at ten past twelve, the crowd had already read what there was to read and gone to lunch. Two first-years lingered with their noses almost against the glass, studying the list the way people study a menu in a restaurant they can't afford. A custodian was wiping the lower pane with a gray cloth.

*1. SHINDOU ITSUKI. 2. SAIONJI REINA.*

He read it once. A year ago those two lines had taken him ten seconds to believe. Now they were information, delivered, like a train timetable. Whatever he had been waiting to feel since April had not arrived and showed no sign of arriving. He pushed his glasses up, then up again, since the first time hadn't done anything, and noticed that his shoulders had come down an inch while he stood there.

She was at the far end of the hall, by the stairs.

Not near the board. Not near him. She had stopped at the opposite wall with her folders held against her chest, in the black blazer she wore on days she intended to be taken seriously, cut to give nothing away and failing at it, as the two boys at the foot of the stairs were demonstrating by reading a notice about bicycle registration with unusual concentration. She was ten meters from him. He estimated it twice and came out the same both times.

"Same as April," Reina said. She didn't raise her voice. The hall's acoustics had always favored people who didn't need them to.

"Yes."

"I want it noted that I have not asked you to come any closer."

"Noted."

"That was a joke. I'm told they land better when the recipient laughs."

"I'm still confirming it was safe."

The corner of her mouth moved, there and gone. She crossed the ten meters in nine paces, he counted, and stopped at the board at a more reasonable two, and read her own name in second place with the same blank courtesy she'd given it in the spring. It didn't crack. It didn't relax either. She looked at it the way you look at a standing appointment.

"I'll beat you next time," she said.

"You said that in April."

"I intend to keep saying it until it is no longer inaccurate."

Yamamoto's email arrived that evening, subject line *Seminar Track: Research Pairings, Autumn Term*, and it said what Yamamoto had already said in person at the library, in the language of a policy: pairs by placement standing, top two first, "in the interest of balanced collaborative output." Each pair was to submit a proposal by Friday, with "sufficient scope for a full term's work."

Itsuki read it on the dormitory stairs with his shoes still on. It was, on paper, a rule. It had one outcome, and he suspected the man who wrote it had enjoyed typing the word *balanced*.

___

Reina arrived on Monday with a folder.

Not a notebook, not a stack of printouts. A folder, labeled, with a colored tab, which she set down between them with the care of someone laying a winning hand. The late sun slanted across the wood. The radiator under the window had not started clanking yet, and wouldn't until the first cold week.

"I've narrowed it to three," she said.

"Three what."

"Proposals. I've ranked them."

"Of course you have."

She opened the folder. Three single-page outlines, identically formatted, each with a working title in bold across the top. She turned the first to face him. "This is the strongest. A comparative reading of precedent in early postwar contract law. It's rigorous, it's thoroughly sourced, and a committee would approve it without needing to be persuaded."

He read it. It was as advertised. It was also, he thought, exactly the paper a committee would want to have already read, the kind of thing a person wrote when she knew what the pages were supposed to weigh before she started.

"It's safe," he said.

"It's sound."

"Those aren't the same word."

She closed the folder half an inch. A warning. He ignored it, turned to the second page, and found her handwriting on it.

*Base-year reform in the local allocation tax.* The title was in the same bold as the others, but the page under it had been marked up in red. Not neat red. Underlined red, bracketed red, a margin note with an arrow and two question marks, ink pressed deep enough to dent the paper.

"You ranked this one second."

"It's less defensible before a committee. The question is political as much as technical, and they'll want a methodological spine I can't yet guarantee."

"There's red under every line of it. There isn't any on the first."

"Red is for questions that remain open."

"The first one has no open questions."

"That is what makes it the strongest."

"That's what makes it finished before you start." He said it as gently as he could, which wasn't very, and slid the page back across. "You ranked by what a committee would approve and underlined by what you'd actually do."

She looked at the page. She didn't answer. For several seconds the only sound at the table was Mori's cart working its way along the stacks two aisles over, a wheel that squeaked once per revolution, until the cart turned the corner and took the noise with it.

"The third is ballast," Reina said finally. "Municipal mergers and demographic projection. Don't read it."

He didn't.

"Do you have an alternative," she said, "or are you here only to critique mine?"

He did. He hadn't intended to bring it up so directly. He had assumed the conversation would run the way their conversations usually ran, she proposing, he agreeing, both of them calling it collaboration. But she was looking at him with the attention she gave to things she expected to win, and something in him, tired of being agreed with, declined to let her.

"Scholarship retention," he said. "Cross-referenced against family income bracket, over the first three years after enrollment. Whether merit-based aid actually predicts long-term academic success, or whether it mostly measures who can afford to stop working while they study."

Reina went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with agreement.

"The registrar publishes aggregate retention by aid category in the annual report," he went on, because the silence needed filling with something solid. "Income brackets we'd have to request through the research office. Yamamoto can sign the request."

"That's more work."

"Probably."

"The data collection alone would take most of the term."

"Probably."

"It would also," she said, laying the sentence on the table like a card turned face up, "be about you."

He did not answer immediately, and was aware of exactly what the delay said in his place.

"It would be about the topic. I have a reason to find it interesting. That isn't the same as it being about me."

"Isn't it."

"No."

She studied him with a look he hadn't seen her use on textbooks. It was the one she'd used across this same table in the spring, back when he was still a problem she hadn't decided whether she liked being confused by. Then she lifted the second proposal, the one in red, and held it up to the window light, as if comparing it against something he couldn't see.

"A funding formula is a funding formula," she said slowly. "Scholarship aid is allocated by a rule. So is a municipal grant. If your question is whether a formula measures what it claims to measure, then it is the same question as mine, with a smaller budget."

"That's yours."

"That is ours, if I am sufficiently convinced." She put the page back in the folder and the folder in her bag, three unused proposals and all. "Send me your outline. I'll tell you what's wrong with it. I expect citations. I do not expect to regret this."

"You say that as if it's already decided."

"It is," Reina said, standing to refill her coffee. "I simply haven't told you why yet."

He didn't ask. He'd learned that the why arrived on its own schedule, days later, wearing something else: a comment, a correction, a container of food he hadn't requested.

___

Kaito told him on Wednesday night, four days after the fact, which for Kaito amounted to fasting.

He had mentioned on Friday, in the voice of a man mentioning surgery, that he had "a thing" on Saturday. Itsuki hadn't asked. Kaito had come back from the thing at eleven p.m. wearing the expression of someone concealing a large animal under a coat, and gone straight to bed, and had been unusually quiet around the dormitory ever since.

Now he sat on the end of his bed with the lamp off, looking at the ceiling.

"Okay," he said. "So. It was good."

"That's it? Four days, and 'it was good.'"

"It was *really* good, man." He didn't look over. "I had a plan. Talking points. We went to the place by the south gate, the one with actual chairs, and I had a whole speech in my head, and then she just asked how my summer was. Not the funny version. The real one." He exhaled. "So I told her. About home, and how every phone call ends with somebody asking what I'm going to do after graduation, and how the honest answer is nothing, a lot of nothing, while everyone else in this building has had a five-year plan since middle school."

"I noticed."

"You're not supposed to notice, you're supposed to be offended I never told you."

"I'm not offended. I'm glad you told her."

Kaito turned his head. In the dark his face was mostly outline, but the attention in it was easy to feel. "She didn't say much back. No 'it'll work out,' none of that. She listened. And somehow that was the whole point." He sat up. "Is that what it's like? Whatever you and Saionji are doing at that table. Is it the listening?"

"We aren't doing anything at that table."

"You're writing a proposal. About your scholarship. That you volunteered. For a seminar that didn't ask you to volunteer anything."

Itsuki said nothing, which Kaito, by now, could read as well as anyone alive.

"Yeah," Kaito said, and lay back down. "That's what I thought."

___

He sent the outline on Thursday night. Three pages, methodology first, the personal stakes absent from every line, exactly as promised. Her reply arrived forty minutes later and ran longer than the outline itself, every paragraph a correction, half of them right.

The last line came on its own.

*Section 3 is underdeveloped. We should discuss in person. Table, Tuesday.*

Not *your* section. *We.*

He typed *Tuesday* and sent it, and sat with the cursor blinking in the empty reply box, unable to find a way to tell her he had read the other word.
