# Chapter 20: Crowd Control

Koyo's cultural festival took over the main quad for two days every November. Itsuki had known that in the abstract since his first year and experienced it properly only now, having spent last year's festival working an extra shift, because the tips from a campus full of visiting families were, according to his manager, obscene.

This year he had the day off, which Kaito had treated as a national holiday.

"Okay, priorities," Kaito said, steering them across the quad with the focus of a man executing a plan he had drafted in advance. "Yakisoba first, because the line triples by noon. Then the haunted house the second-years built, which I'm told is actually upsetting. Then..."

"You have an itinerary."

"I have a *schedule*. There's a difference, and you of all people should respect it." Kaito produced a sheet of paper from his pocket, refolded so many times it had gone soft at the creases. "Yui helped."

"Yui helped you make a schedule for a festival."

"Yui helped because I tried to make one myself and it said 'have fun' four times in a row with no times attached." He said this with no sign of embarrassment, which was one of Kaito's more durable qualities.

They found Yui at the drinks stall the seminar cohort had been guilt-tripped into running, standing behind a folding table with the weary competence of someone who had been there since eight and intended everyone to know it without complaining. The charcoal umbrella leaned against the tent pole beside her. It was not raining. It had not rained in nine days. She had been carrying it everywhere since Wednesday, the way a person carries something she has been told the price of.

"You're late," she said to Kaito, not looking up from the cash box.

"The schedule said eleven."

"The schedule said ten-thirty. You wrote it."

"I wrote it optimistically." He ducked around the table and kissed her on the cheek, quickly, in passing, the way a person does something he has done before and no longer needs an audience for. Six months ago this scene would have been a production, Kaito narrating his own nerves aloud and Yui deflecting with three layers of irony before either of them admitted anything. Now it was a Saturday, and there was a festival.

"You're staring," Yui said to Itsuki.

"I'm observing."

"That's what staring is, with worse posture." She handed him a drink he hadn't ordered, the right one, and went back to the cash box before he could decide whether to be unsettled by that.

___

Reina found him an hour later outside the haunted house, unimpressed by the queue and less impressed by the sign advertising it. She had a navy coat on over a gray sweater and a scarf the color of weak tea, her hair down for once, and three first-years at the next stall had stopped arguing about their tickets to watch her stand in line.

"'Actually upsetting' seems an ambitious claim for a room built by second-years out of cardboard and one fog machine."

"That's Kaito's opinion. Take it up with him."

"I intend to." She studied the food stalls instead, methodically, the way she studied anything she hadn't yet decided about. "What's edible here."

"Depends what you mean by edible."

"I mean I'm not interested in regretting a decision within the hour."

He pointed her toward the stall two down, the one he happened to know from experience cooked the meat through instead of merely browning the outside. She went. When she came back she held two skewers instead of one, and the portion on hers was visibly larger than it should have been, since the man at the grill had apparently decided, without being asked, that she was a person who should have more. She handed the second skewer to Itsuki without a word, as she had been handing him things since October, and neither of them mentioned the arithmetic.

They ate at the edge of the quad, watching the crowd shift and re-form. It took him a minute to notice that neither of them had spoken and neither of them minded.

Across the path, the Political Science Society had converted a lecture hall into a mock Diet. A first-year in a borrowed suit stood at the dais attempting a question to the budget committee. He got the procedure wrong. He put the supplementary question before the principal one, and the volunteer playing the chair, who also did not know, let it stand.

Reina's lips moved. It was the smallest motion, a fraction of a second of the correct order under her breath, and Itsuki would not have seen it if he hadn't already learned where to look.

"They've offered me a turn," she said, a moment later, to nobody. A girl with a clipboard had been hovering at the door. "As a minister. It's supposed to be fun."

"Are you going to take it."

"No." It came back fast, with no space between the question and the answer, and she lifted the skewer and finished the last piece as though that had settled it.

"Kaito made a schedule," he said, mostly to put something into the quiet that wasn't a thought he wanted to keep having.

"I know. Yui showed me. It has a color-coding system."

"You approve."

"I have concerns about the font. The underlying logic is sound." She glanced at him sideways. "You didn't work today."

"No."

"Good," she said, flatly, the way she said most things that mattered, and didn't elaborate, and he found he didn't need her to.

___

He noticed Kuze near the lantern stalls, on the far side of the quad, standing a little apart from the group of third-years he'd arrived with.

He wasn't doing anything. He was watching, the whole loose drifting shape of the afternoon, his attention settling for a moment longer than coincidence on Reina, who was laughing at something Yui had said, her guard down in the way it almost never was in daylight. He did not look like a man admiring someone. He looked like a man measuring a distance.

Then someone called his name, and he turned and smiled and became, in the space of a second, as easy and untroubled as ever, as if the moment before had never happened.

Itsuki didn't know what to do with what he'd seen, so he did what he usually did with things he didn't know what to do with, and went to find out whether the haunted house was, in fact, upsetting.

(It was not. Kaito was inconsolable about this for the rest of the afternoon.)

___

The festival closed, as it did every year, with paper lanterns set adrift on the pond behind the main hall, a tradition nobody could explain the origin of and everybody joined anyway, because it was pretty, and because Koyo liked a ceremony whether or not it meant anything.

Reina wrote something on hers before setting it down, one hand cupped around the candle until it caught, and did not show him what it said. He didn't ask. He wrote his own, short and practical, the kind of thing he wrote on anything, and set it beside hers on the water.

They drifted out together a little way, bumping, and separated. He watched until he could no longer tell which one was his.

Hers he could find the longest. It was the only lantern on the pond with a line of red ink showing through the paper.
