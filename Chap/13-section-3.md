# Chapter 13: Section 3

On Sunday night he asked the manager to take Tuesday's shift off his hands.

The manager was a tired, decent man in his fifties who had long ago stopped asking his part-timers for explanations. He looked at the schedule taped inside the back-room door, at the four hours between one and five on Tuesday morning, and said, "Fine," and Itsuki said, "Schoolwork," which was true in the way most things he told people were true. It was accurate. It left out everything that would have made it interesting.

Four hours at thirteen hundred yen an hour was five thousand two hundred yen. He subtracted it from the running total he kept in his head, the one that governed the phone bill, the food, and the bus fare home at New Year, and went back to restocking the drink case. The total was smaller than it had been in April. He did not think about that.

___

He did the real work on Monday afternoon, between his last lecture and an early bed.

The law library stood across the quad from the main one, a narrow stone building with a reading room that smelled of pencil shavings and old radiator dust and seemed to have been built on the assumption that nobody would ever be hurried. Itsuki had passed it for a year and a half and never once gone in. The Faculty of Law owned it, and he was a student of the Faculty of Law, and the guard at the door checked his ID with the faint surprise of a man who had seen the photo many times and the face never.

The reference desk was staffed by a graduate student in a surgical mask, who looked like someone whose thesis was due and who had been told that people with questions would be arriving. She listened to him for about forty seconds.

"Funding formulas," she said. "Base year."

"Whether the choice of base year has ever been challenged in court. Allocation formulas for local grants, or school funding. The argument would be that it's an administrative choice, so it shouldn't be reviewed."

"That's a better question than most of what I get." She turned a monitor toward him. "Don't search the full text. You'll get four hundred results and every one will mention a budget. Search the headnotes. The summaries somebody was paid to write."

It took him four hours and ten minutes. "Base year" and "allocation" gave him four hundred and twelve hits, as predicted. He added "equal treatment" and got thirty-eight. He added "reasonable basis" and got eleven. Eleven he could read. He read them at a desk under a brass lamp, in the order the database ranked them, and nine were as bad as they had looked. The tenth was almost right.

The eleventh was a ruling from a regional court, a decade old, in a dispute over how a prefecture divided school subsidies among its towns. Five small municipalities had argued that the base year chosen for the formula came before a wave of depopulation, so the formula treated their shrinkage as a permanent fact rather than a loss the subsidy was meant to cushion. The court had not struck the formula down. It had held, in a paragraph he read three times, that the choice of baseline was within the prefecture's discretion only so long as it could be justified by the stated purpose of the subsidy.

A baseline had to explain itself. It was, with the edges rounded off, the argument he and Reina had been having across a ministry report a month earlier, reached from an angle neither of them had known existed.

He made two photocopies at ten yen a page, highlighted them in three colors, and went home. At nine that night he lay down with no alarm set, for a shift that no longer existed, and woke at ten past nine the next morning, the longest he had slept in a month.

___

She was already at the table when he got there at five, with the late sun on the wood and a glass of green tea she brought when she meant to stay a while. He set a laundered lacquer box and a black chopstick case in front of her, side by side.

She opened the case. She checked the chopsticks for scratches under the window light. It took a full five seconds.

"Acceptable," she said, and put both into her bag.

"I didn't know there was a standard."

"There is always a standard." She looked up properly then, and he watched her notice him the way she noticed errors in citations, quickly and without mercy. "You're not doing the two-finger push."

"The what."

"You have two. The knuckle is for disagreement. The two-fingered push is for when you are too tired to focus. I've logged both." She said this to the folder in front of her. "You haven't done either since you sat down. You slept."

"Nine hours."

"Nine hours on a weekday, and you arrived on time. That's a significant variable." She held out a hand, palm up, without looking at it. "Section 3. I said it was underdeveloped, and then you went somewhere."

He slid the folder across. It was thinner than hers, as it always was, and inside it, instead of the outline she'd expected, was a single printed case summary with highlighting in three colors that meant nothing to anyone but him.

She read it. Then she read it again, slower, her pen not moving, the tip of it resting on the page above the paragraph he had read three times himself.

"Where did you find this."

"The law library. A graduate student at the reference desk told me to search the headnotes instead of the full text. Base year and equal treatment, eleven results, one that mattered. It took four hours."

"You went to the law library."

"It's our faculty."

"You have been enrolled in it for a year and a half and never entered the building. I assumed you were allergic to it."

"I don't like research I don't need. This one I needed."

She said nothing for a few seconds, holding the page as though it might rearrange itself if she looked away. "The funding case is unexpectedly relevant," she said at last, in the tone of someone conceding a point she had no intention of calling a concession. "The court's test is that a baseline must be justifiable by the stated purpose of the allocation. Your aid structure has a baseline. The income brackets for eligibility were fixed, if the annual report is accurate, in 2008, and nobody has revised them since."

"They'd need to explain themselves."

"They'd need to explain why a family's income in 2008 is the correct threshold for a household in this decade." She wrote in the margin, in red. "That is a section."

"Does that mean it's any good?"

"It means it's competent. I did not say exceptional."

"I heard what you said."

She didn't push it further, which from her was a compliment. She turned the page toward the light and read it a third time, not because she needed to, he suspected, but because it gave her hands and eyes something to do while whatever she actually felt about it sorted itself out somewhere he couldn't see.

___

They worked through the section for the better part of two hours. She restructured his second paragraph without asking, and was, irritatingly, right to. He caught an error in her citation format and did not gloat, since gloating cost energy, and he had decided to spend what he had on something else. The dictionary lay open between them on a word neither of them fully trusted. Somewhere behind the shelves the radiator gave its first tentative clank of the season, a dry cough, and went quiet again.

At some point she stopped writing notes in the margin of his draft and began writing them in her own. The two documents drifted toward looking like one project instead of two people's homework stapled together. Neither of them mentioned when that had happened.

Her phone lay face down beside the ruler. It had buzzed twice in the first hour, a short insistent vibration against the wood, and she had not turned it over. She had gone on writing in the same even hand, but her jaw had done something small and brief before she smoothed it away, and the quiet that followed was a different one from her thinking quiet. He did not ask. Asking would have cost more than either of them had left at that hour.

"The reading group wants a progress check in three weeks," she said, around seven. "The third-years sit in on those. Apparently it's tradition."

"What counts as a progress check."

"Fifteen minutes. Informal. In front of people who have been at Koyo longer than we have and enjoy nothing more than watching second-years fumble through unfinished work." She squared the pages. "It isn't graded. It simply feels graded, which is worse."

"You'll be fine."

"I know I'll be fine." She stood and slung her bag over one shoulder. "I was thinking of you. You should be rested for it."

She stopped at the end of the table. He had expected her to leave, and she didn't. Instead she took something out of the bag, set it at his elbow, and did not meet his eyes. A bundle in navy cloth, tied in two even loops.

"The recipe yields six," she said, before he could speak.

"You gave me the extra one already."

"That was a different batch. I've started a new one."

She had taken out the bundle first, and then the phone, and he noticed the order of it. The screen lit under her chin, and for a second, before she angled it away, he could read a single word in the sender line. *Mother.*

Her face did not change. Her thumb moved once, and the screen went dark.

"Thursday," she said, to the table, and left.

He stayed where he was. The bundle under his hand was still warm.
