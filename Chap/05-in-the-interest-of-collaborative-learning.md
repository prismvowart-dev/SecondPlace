# Chapter 5: In the Interest of Collaborative Learning

The email came through while Itsuki was still at the register, forty minutes from clocking out.

**[Yamamoto Seminar — Announcement] Term Project: Pair Assignments Posted**

He read the subject line twice before he opened it, on the theory that whatever was inside would still be there in a minute, and he could use the minute.

*Per the arrangement outlined in the syllabus, term project partners for the Advanced Seminar track have been assigned by the presiding faculty member. Assignments are final and will not be reconsidered except in cases of documented conflict.*

Below it, a table. Six pairs, alphabetized with the same clinical indifference as everything else the university's mail server produced. He found his own name on the fourth row, bolded, because whoever built the spreadsheet had been kind enough to do that much.

**SHINDOU, Itsuki — SAIONJI, Reina**

He read it three times. Not because the words changed on a fourth attempt, but because some part of him kept expecting them to.

He closed the email, restocked the drink case because the shift didn't end itself, and reread it twice more before he clocked out.

___

Kaito was awake when he got back, which at that hour meant something had happened.

"Before you say anything," Kaito said, sitting up, phone still in hand, "I want it on record that I didn't do this. Whatever's about to happen, it wasn't me."

"What are you talking about."

"The email. Don't play dumb." He caught the look on Itsuki's face and stopped. "Oh my god. You already know."

"I got the same email you did."

"I didn't get the email. Yui texted me a keyboard smash and four exclamation points, which is an incredible development for us as a friend group, but not the point." Kaito was already out of bed. "You. Her. A project. Assigned by a professor. Do you understand the weight of that sentence?"

"It's a term paper."

"It's fate wearing a syllabus."

"It's a professor filling out a spreadsheet."

Kaito put both hands on his shoulders. "You're going to spend six weeks in a library with Saionji Reina. I've been waiting for a development like this since roughly hour two of knowing you."

"You've known me a month."

"It's been a full month."

___

The seminar met Tuesdays and Thursdays in a room on the third floor of the humanities building that had once been a supply closet and never fully recovered from the demotion. Yamamoto stood at the front of it, glasses pushed up into greying hair, a stack of papers under one arm that looked like it had never once been organized on purpose.

"You've all seen the email," he said, without preamble. "Partners are final. I'm aware some of you have opinions about your partners." His eyes moved briefly toward the back row, where Saionji sat with her hands folded and an expression that could have frozen the room's radiator solid. "I'd encourage you to direct that energy into the project instead."

"Professor Yamamoto." Her voice was perfectly level, which by now Itsuki understood meant the opposite of calm. "May I ask what criteria were used."

"Placement standing from the diagnostic." He was already turning to write something on the board. "The top two in a cohort tend to produce stronger work together than either does alone. It's not a complicated formula."

"There are twelve students in this seminar."

"There are."

"And the top two ended up assigned to each other."

"They did." He capped the marker, considered the board, uncapped it again to fix something. "Efficient use of the roster, Saionji-san. I'd have thought you'd approve of efficiency."

Something in her jaw tightened. She didn't say anything further, and didn't need to. The set of her shoulders did the rest of the work.

___

Class emptied out twenty minutes later. Saionji was standing by the door before Itsuki had finished packing his bag, and she didn't leave.

"I assume," she said, once he reached her, "that your plan is to divide the work by section, complete your half independently, and combine the two the night before the deadline with minimal actual collaboration."

"That wasn't my plan."

"It's the efficient plan."

"It's also not what the assignment's asking for. He wants a joint presentation, not two halves stapled together."

Something flickered across her face, the look of someone who'd prepared a counterargument and had it taken away before she could use it. "You've read the requirements already."

"I read the email three times. Had a shift right after I got it."

She studied him a moment, something between evaluation and reluctant recalibration.

"We'll need to meet. Regularly." She was already retrieving a notebook from her bag, converting whatever this was into a scheduling problem, which he was beginning to suspect was simply how she processed things she didn't want to feel. "I have Tuesdays and Thursdays free after five. The library, third floor, the table by the window. It's usually empty at that hour."

Five o'clock gave him just enough runway to make it to his shift by one, if neither of them ran long. He kept that arithmetic to himself for now.

Itsuki went still.

"That's my table."

"I'm sorry?"

"The one by the window. Third floor. I've been using it since the first week of term."

She looked at him the way a person looks at a piece of evidence that has just, unhelpfully, solved a case she hadn't realized she was working. "There's a folded receipt in the drawer. Used as a bookmark."

"That's not mine."

"It's mine." Something crossed her face, quick, gone before he could read it properly. "I keep a spare box of pencil lead in there as well. A brand I've never seen anyone else buy."

"I noticed."

"So it was you." She said it slowly, fitting it together. "The window was always open a hand's width when I got there. I assumed one of the custodians."

"I get sleepy around four. And I figured whoever else used the table wanted to be left alone."

She looked at the drawer, then at him, and let the coincidence sit there for a beat without comment.

"Tuesday," she said finally, closing the notebook with more force than the moment required. "Five o'clock. Try not to be late."

She left before he could point out that, between the two of them, lateness had never once been his problem.

___

He left the seminar room a few minutes later with a standing Tuesday and Thursday appointment he hadn't exactly agreed to, at a table that had apparently belonged to both of them for a month without either one saying so. He didn't have a word ready for whatever this was going to turn into. He had six weeks to find one.
