# Chapter 22: Second, Again

The cold war held for three more weeks.

Coffee went untouched at every session. The new notebook filled steadily, page after page in her hand alone, and Itsuki stopped mentioning it, because mentioning it the first time had done nothing except cost him something to say. She said good afternoon and good evening, and no name between them. Her wrists stayed still under the table.

He worked the remaining revisions to Section 3 in near silence. She reviewed them, corrected what needed correcting, and said little else. It was a return to something like their first month, before either of them had expected anything of the other beyond competence. He told himself this was fine. He had survived on less for most of his life.

He did not tell himself very convincingly.

The Harrington submissions closed in the second week, on a Friday, without a word from either of them. He knew only because the library emptied early, and a second-year at the next table put his head down on a printout and did not lift it for an hour.

___

The results went up on the Friday of the third week, not on the main hall's board but on a small plaque case outside the seminar room, glass-fronted, far less ceremonial, and taken, he was beginning to understand, at least as seriously by the people who cared.

He had given it no thought since October. He read the plaque without expectation.

**HARRINGTON PRIZE FOR INDEPENDENT RESEARCH**

**Recipient: Shindou Itsuki**

Under it, in smaller type, was the title of his own paper. He had not expected to see it in print. He read the name once, then the title, then the name again.

Somewhere behind the plaque, in a drawer in the scholarship office, a condition had very probably just been met. *Demonstrated research output, as assessed by faculty evaluation.* He registered it as a fact. The relief arrived late and did not stay, because of the person standing a meter to his left.

Reina had been there before him, which he understood half a beat too late meant she had read it first.

She was not looking at the plaque. She was looking at the wall beside it, with the stillness of someone who had rehearsed several versions of this moment and found that none of them matched the one actually happening.

"Congratulations," she said. Her voice was even. It was the same evenness he had heard twenty months earlier outside a different glass case, and it did the same thing to the air around it.

"Saionji-san..."

"You submitted in October."

"Yamamoto said anyone in the track with something worth reading."

"I know what Yamamoto said." She said it without weight, though the weight was there anyway, underneath, as it always was with her. "I submitted one thing. It was not the one that mattered."

He thought of a page marked in red, a title with *base-year reform* on it and a margin note pressed deep enough to dent the paper. He did not ask.

"Saionji-san," he began.

"Don't." It came out sharper than anything she had said to him in three weeks, sharp enough that a first-year passing in the corridor glanced over before thinking better of it. She caught it almost at once, the same quick correction she gave everything. When she spoke again, her voice was level. "I apologize. That wasn't necessary."

"You don't have to apologize."

"I'm aware." She touched the bridge of her glasses with two fingers. "I have somewhere to be."

She didn't. He knew she didn't, because Fridays at this hour had held nothing in all the months he had known her, and she left anyway, at exactly her usual pace, down a corridor that hadn't bothered to part for her this time because there was nobody left in it to do so.

At the foot of the stairs, twelve meters off, a man in a gray-green jacket had stopped. He stood looking at the plaque case as if he had been about to cross to it and decided against it, and when Itsuki met his eyes he nodded, once, and went down.

___

Kaito had heard by dinner, as Kaito heard everything, and greeted it with an enthusiasm that lasted exactly as long as it took him to read Itsuki's face.

"Okay, that's not the reaction of a guy who just won a prize."

"I won a prize."

"You look like you lost one." Kaito put down his chopsticks, and the celebration he'd had ready went out of his face, replaced by something Itsuki rarely saw from him. "What happened?"

"Nothing happened. I won. She didn't. That's the whole story."

"That's not the whole story. You've had that face on for three weeks, since long before any results." Kaito studied him a moment longer than usual. "You going to tell me what's going on, or are we doing the thing where you carry it around alone until it breaks something?"

Itsuki had no answer. Kaito, for once, let it sit, and they ate the rest of dinner in a quiet with more in it than either of them was willing to fill.

___

On Tuesday he went to the library out of habit more than expectation. She was there before him, as always now, notebook open, posture correct.

"Shindou-san," she said, before he had set his bag down. It was the first time she had said his name in three weeks. "I need to apologize. For Friday. And for the three weeks, if I'm being accurate about the timeline."

"You don't need to."

"I do. I was unpleasant to you for reasons that had nothing to do with you, and I let you absorb it because it was easier than explaining." She said it the way she said most difficult things, fast, like pulling a bandage before she could reconsider. "My mother had opinions about my standing. I responded to them the way I always do, by becoming someone less pleasant to be around. You were the nearest person."

He turned what he had over against what he had half guessed. "The family matter. At New Year. You said it wasn't important."

"It isn't." She said it without hesitation. "There is a gathering, and my mother treats gatherings as examinations. There is no one. If the next question is Kuze-senpai, it is not him either. I have spoken to Kuze-senpai twice. I could not tell you anything about him that isn't in the faculty directory." A pause. "I'm telling you because you deserve an explanation. Not because I want to discuss it further."

"Okay."

"That's all. Okay." She looked at him a moment, something unsettled in her face, and then she opened the drawer and set the off-brand pencil beside the dictionary where it lived. She took the old notebook, the soft-spined one, from her bag and put it on the table between them, in the space it had always occupied.

She didn't explain that either. He didn't ask.

He picked up the pencil and wrote one line in the margin of the current page, small and economical, the sort of thing he'd write on anything.

She read it. She said nothing. But she did not close the notebook, and for the rest of the session it sat open between them, half hers and half his. Somewhere in the second hour, under the table, he heard the faint dry sound of her turning her wrists in slow circles, thinking, and she did not appear to know she had started.

It wasn't an apology, on either side, not exactly. It was nearer to a fact both of them had agreed to stop arguing about.

At the far end of the room, a gray-green jacket stopped in the doorway, rested against the frame a moment, and went away again.
