# Chapter 8: The Unwritten Rules

Professor Aihara taught Introduction to Political Thought at nine on Tuesdays, in a tiered hall built for two hundred that was never full when the lecture started and never emptied fast enough when it ended. It was a general-education requirement, open across every faculty. That was how Kaito had ended up in the seat beside Itsuki. He studied business, and had never once expressed interest in political thought as a category of human knowledge.

"Best credit-to-effort ratio on campus," Kaito had said, by way of explanation. "Everyone says so."

Aihara himself was a thin man in a cardigan gone soft with washing, chalk permanently ground into both sleeves, who spoke quietly enough that the back rows had long since decided he was background noise rather than a professor. This Tuesday he was three weeks into an argument that a government lasts only as long as the people underneath it keep agreeing to pretend it should. Itsuki had read the assigned chapter on his break the night before. He was actually following it, right up until the projector fan, the chalk-dry air, and four hours of sleep reached an agreement without consulting him.

His pen slid a slow line down the page. His chin dropped after it.

Kaito's elbow found his ribs just before someone three rows down called out, "Is this going to be on the midterm?"

Aihara stopped mid-sentence.

He took off his glasses, looked at them, then at the hall, and the back rows, loud on every other day of the semester, went quiet.

"Thirty-one years," he said. "I have spent thirty-one years on the question of why anyone should obey anyone else. And somewhere between my desk and your notebooks, it apparently became a course you take to round out your credits." There was no heat in it. He said it the way a man reads out a measurement. "I don't say this to shame anyone. I would simply like it said out loud once, so that I know I noticed."

Nobody moved. Then he put his glasses back on, said, "Page forty-one," and the rustle of two hundred people resumed as if none of it had happened at all.

"Well," Kaito whispered. "That's the most honest thing I've heard in this building all year."

Itsuki was fully awake by then. In the front row, dead center, one head hadn't turned to whisper to a neighbor about it. Saionji was writing the sentence down, word for word, in the margin of her notes.

When the hall let out, she went down the steps against the flow of everyone else instead of with it, and stopped at the lectern before Aihara had finished gathering his papers. Itsuki watched from the aisle, in no hurry to leave, as she opened her book to a page she'd already marked and asked something about a footnote near the bottom of it. Aihara listened with his head tilted, the posture of a man not used to follow-up questions. Then something in his face opened up, and he started talking with both hands, sketching an idea in the air that had nothing to do with page forty-one.

"She's the only one who asked him anything," Kaito said, watching with the frank interest of someone watching a nature documentary.

"She's the only one who read it."

"You read it."

"I read it on my break. I fell asleep during the lecture. Those are different achievements."

___

By the fourth week, the schedule had grown a third day without either of them formally agreeing to it. Saionji added a Saturday slot to her spreadsheet, ten in the morning, and informed him of it rather than asked. He countered with two in the afternoon, and she looked at him long enough to confirm he wasn't going to explain the reason, then changed the cell without asking a second time.

The reason was the register. Saturday mornings were the tail end of his overnight shift, and there was no version of ten a.m. that didn't put him at a library table three hours after clocking out, running on whatever his body had scraped together standing behind a counter. He'd learned by the second week that two in the afternoon was the difference between being merely tired and being someone who occasionally forgot what year it was.

He hadn't explained any of that to her. He'd simply moved the number.

On the Friday between the second and third week, he stood in front of the student employment board in the administration building longer than its contents strictly warranted.

*LIBRARY SHELVING. Weekdays, 1–5 p.m. ¥1,000/hr. 12 hrs/wk.*

He did the arithmetic standing up, the same arithmetic he ran on most things that mattered: forty-eight thousand a month, against the seventy he needed clear before anything else got paid, against the night shift's much higher total but far worse hours. It didn't work, not on paper. He photographed the posting anyway, in case the numbers changed their mind later in the semester, and went to class.

___

By then the table had rules nobody had ever said out loud. He brought two coffees on the way in and she never once thanked him for the second, though she always drank it. Her steel ruler lived on the right side of the folders and his notebook lived on whatever was left. The library's ancient dictionary, spine more broken-in than either of their own copies at home, had migrated from the reference shelf to their table sometime in the second week and never migrated back. Nobody discussed whose job it was to return it. Nobody intended to.

The third-floor window had started going dark earlier by the week, the color of early autumn arriving before either of them had thought to mention summer leaving. It was, by then, just the light the table came with.
