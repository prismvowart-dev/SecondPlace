# Chapter 14: Progress Check

Her mother called at eight o'clock on the first Tuesday of the month. Reina had told him that once, in the spring, in the voice she used for train timetables, and a person who knew it arranged to be somewhere she could answer.

She took it standing. Two steps toward the stacks, so the table was out of her line of sight, and her free hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding something in place. Itsuki didn't listen. He didn't have to. Her half of the call reached him anyway across the quiet of the third floor: the voice that climbed half a register into something more careful than her usual, the words *Yes, Mother*, twice in the same flat cadence, and a third time that came out smaller than either.

She had not mentioned the message three weeks ago, the word on the screen. He hadn't asked.

She came back and sat down as if nothing had happened.

"Is everything all right?"

"Fine." She turned a page. "She wants me home for the New Year gathering. There is a family matter to settle." A pause, brief and clean. "It isn't important. We have a presentation in three hours."

He let it go. He read the same line of his notes four times.

She had dressed for the room: a dove-gray jacket over a white blouse, cut close and plain, hair pinned up off her neck, the thin frames set on her face as though they'd been poured there. Twice during the read-through he saw her touch the bridge of them with two fingers, steadying them rather than seeing through them, and neither time did she seem to know she'd done it.

___

The reading group met in a lecture hall far too large for the people using it. Third-years sat scattered through the first rows with the loose posture of people who had survived this exercise once and now came for the entertainment. Yamamoto had taken the third row on the aisle, a stack of papers leaning on his knee. Yui was two seats from the front, having entered under a claim of being Reina's guest that Reina would later describe as an overstatement.

Kaito was in the corridor.

He had come as far as the doors with a thermos of barley tea, where a second-year with a clipboard had informed him that the group was open to the seminar track and invited guests, and he was neither. He stood outside with the grave dignity of a man refused entry to a funeral, and lifted the thermos at Itsuki through the glass panel in the door, a toast.

Two pairs went before them. The first stumbled through a methodology the presenters clearly did not agree on, one correcting the other mid-sentence in a whisper the microphone caught. The second read directly off their slides in a monotone so even that Yamamoto began checking his watch with open sincerity.

"We're going to look competent," Reina murmured, "purely on the basis of making eye contact."

"Low bar."

"I intend to clear it by a wide margin."

___

She went to the lectern. The hall's attention rearranged itself around her in the small, automatic way it did, third-years shifting forward in their seats, pens stopping, the tall windows laying a bar of light across the gray of her jacket. She touched her glasses once, took a breath he could see from the front row, and began.

"A fixed share of the scholarship students admitted to Koyo leave before they graduate. We want to know whether the money finds the people who will stay, or only the people who can afford to."

No notes. Her voice reached the back wall without being raised. She laid out the question in four sentences, the legal frame in three, and then stepped half a pace left to clear the lectern for him.

He didn't move.

He had worked until five that morning and been awake since eight, a sum he did not do in front of people, and for two full seconds his eyes stayed on the page in his hand and his feet stayed where they were. The room waited. Somewhere behind him a chair creaked.

"Shindou-san," Reina said, quietly, without turning her head. "The figure."

He clicked. The slide came up, two curves diverging on a grid, and the floor tilted back under him.

"The registrar publishes retention by aid category. Students on partial tuition aid leave at about the expected rate. Students on full tuition aid leave in their third year at roughly one and a half times that rate." His voice came out steadier than the last two seconds had earned. "That's the opposite of what the merit rationale predicts. If the scholarship selects for the strongest students, the strongest group should be the one that stays. We don't yet have the income brackets. The request is with the research office. The pattern is already visible."

After that it went the way it went at the table. She supplied the case name before he reached it, he spoke the empirical half of a sentence she had built the legal half of, and when she stepped back to hand him the next slide he was already holding the clicker out to her, wrong hand, and they both pretended it was a joke.

A third-year in the second row raised a hand, a tall girl with a pen behind each ear. "Your court test says a baseline is justified by the stated purpose of the allocation. But the stated purpose of this aid is excellence, and excellence is what retention measures. Isn't that circular?"

It was a good question. He felt the room feel it.

"Only if the purpose is defined by the outcome," Reina said. "The scholarship's founding statute defines it by access, and retention is the evidence that access was achieved—"

"We can't say that yet," Itsuki said.

It came out before he had decided to say it. A silence opened, the kind with a floor in it. He watched her not turn her head.

"We cannot say that yet," Reina agreed, in the same even voice, "which is the reason the request is with the research office." A beat. "Shindou-san has the timing."

"The retention gap appears before the third-year exam cycle," he said. "Which is when most full-aid students pick up an additional job. We can't claim causation. But the timing doesn't look like academic failure. It looks like arithmetic."

The girl wrote something down. Yamamoto, in the third row, asked nothing, which from him was applause, and made one note on his pad that Itsuki was fairly sure had nothing to do with funding precedent.

When they finished, the hall stayed quiet for exactly as long as it takes people to decide to clap, and then clapped.

___

The man who caught them in the corridor had ink on his right cuff and was a little out of breath, as though he had taken the stairs two at a time to arrive before the room emptied.

Tall. Black hair kept short and swept back. A gray-green jacket, and on the lapel a small enamel pin of two crossed bamboo swords. He crossed the hallway toward them without hesitation and stopped one pace nearer to Reina than the corridor required.

"Saionji." No honorific. His eyes went to her hands first, then her face, which was not the order people usually used. "That was well done. The precedent argument especially."

"Thank you," Reina said, with the polite blankness she kept for compliments from people she hadn't classified. "I'm sorry. You are?"

His smile didn't leave. It held a fraction longer than a smile does when it's meant, like a man waiting for the rest of a sentence. "Kuze Shun. Third-year, politics track."

"Saionji Reina."

"I know," he said. Then, easily, the eyes coming across to Itsuki, with a nod that was not unkind: "Good work, both of you."

"Thanks," Itsuki said.

Kuze was already turning back to Reina before the word had landed. "I'll see you at the department mixer next week."

It wasn't a question. It was delivered with the calm of a man who had never needed to wonder whether an invitation would be accepted.

"Perhaps," Reina said.

From her, that was not a no. Itsuki noticed that too.

Kuze left. Down the corridor Kaito was raising his thermos at the nearest passerby, announcing a toast to a presentation he had watched through glass. Yui had come out behind them and stood with her hands in her sleeves, watching Reina's face with the quiet attention of a woman keeping score.

Itsuki pushed his glasses up with one knuckle. It was the gesture Reina had told him was for disagreement, and he stood in the corridor with his hand at his face, searching, and found there was nothing to disagree with.
