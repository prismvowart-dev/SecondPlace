# Chapter 37: Four Points

*(Saionji Reina, December)*

On the evening of the third of December she came out of the hospital at 7:20 with a plan to walk to the station, and the black car was at the curb.

It stood on the far side of the road with its engine running, a long, low, unhurried car of the kind that waited outside her family's house at New Year, and the driver at the wheel wore gloves. She had seen him before, in November, at the dormitory gate, when he had stood at the open rear door with his eyes on the pavement and waited for her to get in. She had got in. One did, with that car.

She did not walk to the station now. She stood under the hospital's awning and let him see her see him.

He did not move. After a moment she understood that she was not the reason he was there. Nobody had come out to her. Nobody lowered a window. He was simply parked, as a man is parked who has been told to watch something and has not been told when to stop.

She took out the red pen.

*4. The car, 7:20, Dec 3. Outside the ward.*

She underlined it once, because four was the threshold, and then she walked to the station with the pen still in her fist.

___

Her mother telephoned on the Sunday after, in the evening slot reserved for matters of weight, and for the first minute neither of them mentioned the thing they had both rung about.

"You are well," Miho said.

"Yes."

"You are not sleeping."

"I sleep enough."

"That is the answer of someone who is not." A pause, set down carefully. "I am told the boy is recovering."

Reina's grip changed on the phone. She had not told her mother anything. She had not told anyone in her family anything, and she knew exactly which of her aunts attended which mah-jong table. "Who told you that?"

"I am not without sources, Reina. I have been your mother for twenty-one years." Another pause. "I understand there is an issue with his scholarship."

"There is an issue with a deadline, which falls inside a shutdown. It can be fixed by one signature, and the person who holds it will not be in the country until January." She had not intended to say all of it. "I need a route. There is a member of the welfare committee. He owes this family a courtesy, which I believe you know. I am asking you to collect it."

The silence on the line was of a quality she had learned in childhood to read as arithmetic.

"What do you offer," her mother said.

"I'm sorry?"

"You are asking me to spend something. I am asking what you are prepared to pay. You do not, as I recall, ask anyone for anything unless you have already decided what it costs you." It was not unkind. It was an observation, the way a surgeon observes a wound. "Your grandfather's New Year gathering is on the second of January. You have told me you will appear for the hour that courtesy requires and leave. You will stay the full four hours, in the green, and you will be civil to whomever you are placed beside. In exchange, I will make one telephone call."

"One."

"I do not make two."

Reina thought about it for the length of one breath. It was a fair price. That was the worst of it, that she could not find the error in it. Her mother had offered exactly what she would herself have asked, and had priced it so that refusing would look like vanity.

"I will wear what I own," she said.

"That is the green."

"Then I will wear the green."

"Good." Her mother's voice did not change, but something behind it did, small and quickly smoothed. "I will say one more thing and then I will end this call. Your grandfather asked his assistant in September for a file on your seminar. I know because I was in the room when it was delivered. It is thick. I do not know what he intends. He does not tell me what he does, Reina. He informs me when it is done."

She ended the call there, as she always did, without goodbye. Reina sat looking at the dark phone for some time.

*5*, she wrote, below the underline, and then did not write anything after it, because a fifth point that had been handed to her was a gift, and she did not trust gifts that fit.

___

She trained every morning. It was the only column in the week that had not been disturbed.

The annex gym kept its cold iron smell and its single working row of lights, and she arrived at 5:35 and chalked her hands and went through the block she had written for herself in March, and her body did what it was told. She held the planche on the twenty-first of December for forty seconds. That was the number at the top of the page, the number she had set herself in the spring and meant to be early for. She came down from it and stood with her palms white and her pulse level and felt nothing at all.

She wrote *40* in the log. The log she showed her family said *Suspended* in her own hand, under a date in November. The other one lived in a different place.

She looked at the number for a while. She had expected something. It seemed to her that a number you had worked towards for nine months ought at least to produce a flicker, some small sign that the machine behind the mirror had registered the event, and there was none. The achievement sat on the page like a figure in someone else's accounts.

*Satisfaction*, she tried. *Absent.* It was not a very funny entry, and she did not finish it.

___

On the thirty-first she went to the empty gym at noon, with the building shut and the caretaker away, and stood in the middle of the mat and said the question aloud.

"Did you, or anyone acting on your behalf, cause Shindou Itsuki to be struck by a car."

It echoed once off the far wall and came back with the length of the sentence on it.

Too long. A man like her grandfather would let her finish the question and then answer a better one. She took a pencil from her bag and a card, and wrote it out, and crossed it out, and wrote a second version.

*Did you arrange the accident.*

Too vague. He would ask which accident.

*Did you have him hit.*

She looked at it for some time. It sounded like something a child would ask. It had no subordinate clause in which an elderly man could shelter. It required a single syllable, or a refusal to give one, and she would know which from the way his hands lay on the table.

She decided that was an advantage.

She tore the card in half and put the pieces in her pocket, where the key to a room in another house still sat, and went to collect the green.
