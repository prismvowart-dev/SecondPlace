# Chapter 31: Worse Than Me

Visiting hours opened to non-family at six on the evening of the third, and at two minutes past, someone stopped in the doorway of room 412.

He knew who it was before he turned his head, from the quality of the stopping.

She was in a long coat that hung open over a gray training top, her hair tied back with a plain band instead of pinned, one strand already working loose at the temple. Her glasses sat a few degrees off true. There were shadows under her eyes of a depth that did not come from one bad night, and along the creases of her knuckles, where she had clearly scrubbed, a thin pale line remained.

"You look worse than I do," Itsuki said. "I'm the one with the metal rod."

Something crossed her face, quick, and was put away. "I have not been sleeping well."

"I got hit by a car and slept fine."

"That is not the same thing."

"No," he agreed.

She came in as far as the foot of the bed and stopped there with her arms folded, the posture of someone who had walked in prepared to deliver a finding and had found the room arranged differently. He waited. He had learned that waiting got further with her than pushing did.

"You sent four words," she said. "The procedure specified one."

"It was accurate."

"It was acceptable." She looked at the sill. "Those are very well arranged."

"I'm told someone left them at the desk. There was a thumbprint on the slip. White."

"That is circumstantial."

"Your sense of circumstance is very selective," he said, to the ceiling, and she made a sound that could have been the beginning of a laugh and was at once corrected into a cough.

She sat in the visitor's chair. It was a molded plastic thing, and she sat in it the way she sat in everything, as though it had been issued to her. For a while neither of them said anything. Down the corridor a trolley went by with one bad wheel.

"Does it hurt," she said.

"Not unless I laugh."

"Then do not."

"I'll do my best."

"I was informed on the thirtieth," Reina said, to her own hands, "that the ward was family only. I stood at the desk for two hours. I did not enjoy being informed." She turned her wrist over and looked at the pale line, as though it were someone else's. "I have since read the regulations. Section 4.2 governs absence beyond the permitted threshold. A hardship exemption must be filed within thirty days of the qualifying event. You are at day three."

"I know."

"Then we file."

"It needs my signature, in person."

"I am aware of that as well." She looked up. "I do not accept 'the window is closed' as a finding. I will find the route."

He considered saying that it was his problem, and that there was no route, and that the arithmetic had already told him the shape of the answer on the second day. He looked at her instead, at the crooked glasses and the strand of hair, and said, "Thank you."

"I said it was not a favor." She stood, and tucked the loose strand back with one fingertip, slowly, and it came undone again. "I will return tomorrow."

"My mother's arriving."

"Then the day after."

"You don't have to."

"I am aware of what I have to do," Reina said, and left at exactly her usual pace.

___

His mother came on the fourth, off the night bus at dawn, and spent the first ten minutes fixing things that did not need fixing. She straightened the blanket at the foot of the bed twice. She refolded a stack of hospital pamphlets into a neater stack. She did not cry, which he understood as its own effort.

"You look thin," she said, as she said after every injury and every holiday.

"There isn't much walking in a hospital bed."

"That isn't what I meant and you know it." She sat in the molded chair with her hands folded in her lap, as if she had practiced sitting calmly somewhere around Nagoya. "The doctor said six weeks. Maybe eight."

"That's what they told me."

"And the store."

"They're holding my hours. Not paying them. Holding."

"And the scholarship."

He did not answer that one any faster than he would have for anyone, and his mother, who had read his silences for twenty years, did not make him try twice.

"I have some savings," she said carefully, like a sentence rehearsed on the bus. "Not much. If there is a gap..."

"There won't be one you need to cover."

"Itsuki."

"I'll handle it."

She looked at him, the look she had given him at seven when he insisted on carrying both grocery bags up four flights and his arms had shaken. "You've been handling things since you were twelve," she said. "I would like, once, to be allowed to handle something too."

He had no reply to that, and for once the silence was not a win.

___

Kaito came back from the administration building on the ninth wearing the posture of a man who had lost an argument he had been certain he would win.

"So," he said, dropping into the chair. "You can't file a leave-of-absence extension on behalf of another human being. Even with the doctor's note. Even asking nicely. Even asking less nicely, and then cornering a clerk who clearly agreed with me and said no anyway."

"It needs a registered seal and a signature in front of a registrar."

"Or a notarized proxy, which requires a notary, who does home visits, who is booked solid until January eighth." Kaito held up a hand before Itsuki could do the sum. "And the registrar closes on the twenty-seventh and reopens on the sixth. I looked at the thirty-day line and almost threw the form at the wall. Day thirty is the thirtieth of December. It falls in the middle of the shutdown."

"Someone designed that."

"Someone who has never needed it." Kaito rubbed his face. "I'm sorry, man. I tried."

"I know you did."

___

Yamamoto called on the eleventh, which was unusual enough that Itsuki answered before the second ring finished.

"I made some calls," Yamamoto said, without preamble. "There is a hardship provision. I have used it twice in nine years, successfully, for exactly this kind of thing. I am told that this year it needs a dean's signature, and the dean is abroad until the ninth of January. The fund has also been reallocated." A dry pause. "I am the second most senior person in that department and I could not get past the first line of it. I wanted you to hear that from me rather than from a letter."

"I understand."

"I don't think you do, not all of it, and that's all right. I'm not finished trying." Another pause, the kind Yamamoto used instead of comfort. "I have had a great many students through that seminar room. You were never going to need luck to justify being there. This is the first time luck has had anything to do with it, and it has gone badly. That is not the same as deserving it."

He hung up before Itsuki could find anything to say, which was probably why he had ended the call there.

The phone buzzed again within a minute.

**Reina:** *Thursday. Six o'clock. I will have a route. You will not like it.*

He looked at that for some time, from a bed in which he had still not succeeded in sitting up.
