# Chapter 6: The Table by the Window

Itsuki reached the third floor at four fifty-two and found her already there.

She had taken the chair with its back to the radiator, which left him the one facing the window, and she had colonized about two thirds of the table before he arrived. Four folders fanned out by color. A steel ruler, for reasons he decided not to ask about. A laptop open to a document whose table of contents ran longer than some papers he had handed in. Her hair was pinned up in a low twist with a plain black clip, and she sat very straight, like someone who had been trained to and never stopped.

Beside the laptop stood a stack of books. Most had the library's blue spines and a seminar reading slip tucked inside the cover. Two did not. One was a fat volume with a cracked binding, *A Working Guide to the Budget Process*, the kind of book nobody read for pleasure. The other lay underneath it, spine turned to the table, no title showing.

"You're early," she said, without looking up.

"So are you."

"I'm always early." She squared a folder against the table edge, then squared it again. "Sit. I'd like to start with an outline."

He sat in the chair he had used for a month and put his notebook down in the sliver of table she hadn't claimed. One notebook, one pen.

"I was going to start with a thesis and see where it went."

"And if it goes somewhere wrong?"

"I'd notice around page three."

"Page three is late." She turned the laptop toward him. "Every section mapped before either of us writes a sentence, so we aren't duplicating each other."

The first folder had three tabs. *Primary sources. Secondary sources.* And, in the same neat hand, *Sources I'm still deciding about.*

"How long did the color-coding take?"

"An evening." Now she did look at him, and at the single notebook, like an auditor going through a stranger's luggage. "That's everything you brought?"

"I read the material on my break."

"On your break." She let it go, visibly, and returned to the screen.

___

For the next hour they barely spoke. It was two operations running side by side at one small piece of furniture: her pen moving in short, controlled strokes across a legal pad, his notebook filling in the looser, faster hand he had learned writing between customers. The radiator, which spoke once an afternoon at four, had nothing to add.

At the next table, two first-years had arranged textbooks and pens into a convincing display of studying and were not doing any. One of them kept glancing over.

"They'll lose interest by Thursday," Reina said, still writing.

"You noticed them."

"They've been looking since you sat down." She turned a page. "There's nothing to see."

Itsuki read the same paragraph of the case review for the third time. It had been happening more this week than he liked: the words arriving fine and then sliding off. He pushed his glasses up without looking and went back to the top of the paragraph. Across the table, her pen paused for exactly as long as it took his hand to come down, then went on.

The case was the Nagase prefectural school-funding reform. Rural districts had been promised a guaranteed floor, and within two budget cycles the shortfalls had ended the whole scheme. The official review ran forty pages and put the blame on "local administrative capacity."

"Eleven years," Reina said. It was the first thing she had said in twenty minutes that wasn't about logistics, and she had stopped writing to say it. "Eleven years of shortfalls in the smallest districts, and the review calls it a capacity problem. A village of nine hundred people was supposed to make up for how the formula rounds."

He hadn't expected the heat in it. It was more than the assignment called for, and she seemed to hear that herself. She picked up the pen again and her voice came back down to level.

"It's the enrollment formula," he said. "The rest is downstream of that."

"I agree. But I want the chain written out. Shortfall, then reallocation, then the incentive that produced the reallocation. Otherwise it's an opinion with a citation attached."

"I got there in four minutes. If it's that obvious, writing out six steps is decoration."

"You got there in four minutes because you already knew where you were going. The committee hasn't read your mind." She said it flatly, without malice. "They grade the reasoning."

He considered that, found it was right, and did not enjoy the discovery. He turned his notebook around and slid it across the table: four lines, arrows between a handful of terms, in a shorthand that made sense to exactly one person.

She read it from the last arrow back to the first, lips pressed flat.

"This isn't my reasoning," she said slowly.

"No."

"You started at the formula and went forward. I started at the shortfall and worked back to the formula." She tapped her own page, then his. "Different roads. Same place."

"Then it probably holds."

"Or we're both wrong in the same direction." But she was already copying his four lines into her chain, adding what hers lacked, and the corner of her mouth had shifted a few degrees without her seeming to know it.

His eyes drifted to the cracked-binding book while she wrote.

"It isn't on the syllabus," she said, not looking up.

"I didn't say anything."

"You looked." She kept writing. "It's useful to know how a formula gets written before you argue about why it failed."

"Who wrote this one?"

"The review doesn't say. Which is the first thing I'd like to know."

Some time after that she rolled her wrists and shoulders back, quick and controlled, the way someone does who trains and doesn't mention it, and went on writing.

___

At some point the windows had gone dark. The woman from the front desk, cardigan, laminated badge that said MORI, came through switching off the green-shaded reading lamps nobody was using. She stopped at their table a beat longer than the task needed, took in the two of them and the fanned-out folders, and moved on without a word.

Itsuki checked his phone. 9:58.

"I have to go."

Reina looked up. "It's not even ten."

"I know."

She waited for the rest of the sentence. It didn't come. He zipped his bag and put on his jacket.

"Thursday," she said. "Five. Bring more than one notebook."

"I'll consider it."

He took the stairs two at a time and did the arithmetic on the second landing. Two hours of sleep before the register, if he skipped dinner.

He skipped dinner.
