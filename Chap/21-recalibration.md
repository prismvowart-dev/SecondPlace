# Chapter 21: Recalibration

She was already at the table when he arrived on Tuesday, which wasn't unusual in itself, except that she had arranged her things so that no space remained on his side, and she did not look up when he sat down.

"Saionji-san."

"Good afternoon." She did not say his name, and he found that he had been counting on it. "I've moved the Section 3 revisions to Thursday. I have other priorities this week."

"Other priorities."

"The Harrington deadline is in three weeks." She said it to the page in front of her, in the voice she used for facts rather than conversation. "I intend to submit something worth submitting."

He knew the Harrington the way he knew most of the university's ceremonial architecture, a name on a plaque, an award given once a year to whichever student's independent research the faculty judged best, prestigious in the way that meant everyone claimed not to care and a handful of people had organized their whole year around it anyway. He hadn't known she was one of them. He hadn't known, until this moment, that he wasn't supposed to have known.

He had a submission of his own sitting in the Harrington office. He had handed it in during October on Yamamoto's say-so, an extension of the funding paper written in the gaps and forgotten. He considered telling her. The way she had said *worth submitting* decided it.

"You never mentioned it."

"There wasn't a reason to." She turned a page with more care than the action called for. "There is now."

He set the coffee down anyway, out of habit more than hope. She said "Thank you" in a voice that made the two words sound like a form being correctly observed, and did not touch the cup for the rest of the session.

It was nothing he could have pointed at. Her hair was pinned, her jacket was gray, her pen moved in the same short upright strokes. But under the table, where for six months she had rolled her wrists in slow circles whenever she was thinking, her hands were folded in her lap and still. He noticed it the way he noticed things about her, immediately, and had nowhere to put it.

___

On Thursday he noticed the notebook.

Not the one he knew, soft-spined, dog-eared, a term of two hands arguing across the same pages, but a new one, spine still stiff, cover unmarked, the kind of notebook a person bought to begin something over. She wrote in it in her usual hand and nothing else. No margin left open. No space that looked as though it were waiting.

He didn't ask about it. Asking would have meant naming what he was seeing, and naming it would have meant admitting how much it cost to see it, and he had a long, well-practiced history of declining to do either.

"You have a new notebook," he said, which was as close as he let himself get.

"The old one was full."

It was not full. He knew with the useless certainty of someone who had personally written a third of its pages that it still had a few dozen blank sheets at the back. He did not correct her. Correcting her would have meant admitting he'd counted.

"All right," he said, and let it go, because letting things go was the one skill he'd had a full year's head start on before he ever met her, and it was, apparently, still the only one he could produce on command.

___

"She's being weird," he told Kaito that night, which was as much of an admission as he was prepared to make aloud, to anyone, about anything.

"Define weird."

"Formal. Distant. She hasn't said my name in a week."

Kaito, to his credit, didn't laugh, which was new. "Did something happen?"

"I don't know."

"Did you ask?"

"No."

"Why not?"

Itsuki had no answer for that, which Kaito, for once, let stand. He watched Itsuki with an expression that had less chaos in it than usual and more of something Itsuki didn't have a name for and didn't ask about either.

"You should probably ask," Kaito said, quietly, uncharacteristically simply. "As a thought. From someone who spent four months not asking Yui things and regretting all of it."

"It's different."

"Sure," Kaito said, in the tone of a man who didn't believe that and had decided, for tonight, not to say so.

___

By the following week the coffee sat untouched three sessions running. The new notebook had filled a third of its pages in her hand alone, and Itsuki had begun arriving at the table with the careful stillness of a man approaching something he suspected, correctly, he had no permission to touch.

On the Thursday he opened the drawer, out of an old habit he did not stop to examine. The dictionary lay where it lived. The folded receipt was still there.

The spare pencil, the off-brand one, was gone.
