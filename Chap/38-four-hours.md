# Chapter 38: Four Hours

*(Saionji Reina, the second of January)*

The green was a kimono her mother had chosen when Reina was fifteen and had had altered twice since. It was dark and plain and cut for a girl who held her shoulders back as a matter of habit, and Reina wore it into her grandfather's house at one o'clock on the second of January with the two halves of an index card in her sleeve and her hands folded in front of her, like a woman delivering a document.

The long room had been opened to its full length. Forty people knelt along two tables in the order the family kept them in, and she was shown to a cushion next to an old friend of her grandfather's.

His name was Harada. He was sixty-three, retired from a bank, polite, with a trimmed white moustache and the bright, unforced manner of a man who had never had to prove he deserved the room he was in. He bowed to her at the correct angle and said how glad he was, and then, within the first six minutes, began to tell her about golf.

She listened. She had agreed to four hours, and she kept agreements in full.

He described a bunker on a course in Chiba. He described the cost of the membership, and a hole called the Widow, and a morning in October, three years after he had taken the game up, when he had reached the green in two and then taken four putts. She asked one question about how a handicap was calculated, since it was a system with rules, and he explained it with such evident pleasure that she found herself following the arithmetic with real interest. He was a perfectly agreeable man, and she was obliged to be agreeable back.

At the head of the table her grandfather sat upright and ate very little. Twice in the first hour his eyes moved down the room to her, rested for exactly as long as courtesy allowed, and moved on. At the far end, her mother did not look at her once, which in Miho was a form of looking.

The clock above the alcove said 2:50. Two hours and ten minutes remained.

She thought of a hospital desk, and a plastic chair, and a man in a pressed suit bowing at the correct depth. She thought of the black car with its engine running. She kept her back straight and her hands folded and said, "A four-putt on the Widow. That must have been very trying," and Harada laughed with delight and agreed that it had.

___

At five the guests began to rise, and the long room filled with the quiet clatter of people collecting coats from a house that had stopped being warm. Harada bowed once more and said that he hoped they would meet again. Reina said that it had been educational. He took it as a compliment, and she let him.

When the room had thinned, she crossed it and stopped in front of her grandfather's place at the table.

"Grandfather. May I have a word."

He looked up. His expression gave away nothing; it never had. "Now?"

"If it is convenient."

He rose, which took him a moment, and led her through the paper door into the small room at the back that had been his study for thirty years. He did not sit. Neither did she. The door slid closed behind them.

She had rehearsed the question in an empty gym for two days and had never once rehearsed what her face would do, and she let it do nothing. She looked at his hands, because she had been taught as a child that a man's hands told the truth long before his mouth did.

"Did you have him hit?"

For a moment there was no sound in the room at all. She heard a clock in the hall, and the cold tick of the stove, and under both the dry, even sound of her own breath.

His hands lay flat on the front of his hakama. They did not move. Then, at the last word, the left one closed, slowly, around a fold of cloth, and opened again.

"I did not," her grandfather said.

She had expected a pause, an evasion, a better question offered in place of hers. She had not expected two words, spoken at the volume of a man reading a figure aloud.

She studied his face. She looked for the thing that would separate a guilty man from a falsely accused one, the flicker, the fraction of delay, and found that she was looking at exactly what a man would look like in either case. It was the look of someone who had been hit across the face with a fact. A well-briefed man would have worn it. An innocent man would have worn it. She had spent a month learning that she could not tell the difference, and the knowledge sat in her chest like a stone.

"Thank you for answering," she said.

She bowed. It was the bow she had been taught at six, held for a count of four, executed from the hips with the back flat, and she saw him register it, because he was the person who had taught it to her.

"Reina."

She had her hand on the paper door.

"Whatever it is that makes you ask that," her grandfather said, and she heard his voice move out of its level, a quarter-tone and no more, a thing she had never once heard it do, "I would like to know what it is."

"I did not say I believed it."

"You did not need to."

"Then I have asked, and you have answered," Reina said. "That is the whole of the exchange." She slid the door open. "Thank you for the gathering. It was very well done."

She had expected the corridor to feel different. It did not. It smelled of cold stone and of incense from the hall shrine and of a hundred New Years she had stood through with her hands folded, and she walked down it at her ordinary pace, past the photograph that hung where it had always hung, two names in red ink gone brown, and did not turn her head.

___

Her mother was waiting in the entrance hall, with Reina's coat over her arm.

"You asked him," Miho said.

It was not a guess. Reina took the coat. "Yes."

"What did he say."

"That he did not."

Her mother's eyes went to the study door, which was closed, and back. In her face something happened that Reina had not seen there before and could not name. "I will make the call tomorrow," Miho said. "It does not depend on what has happened in this house this afternoon. I wish you to know that."

"I know."

"I do not make two," her mother said, "but I keep the one."

Reina inclined her head, the small formal nod she gave to a person she respected and could not afford to thank. Then she put on her shoes and went out.

The car was already at the foot of the steps. It had been summoned the way it was always summoned, without a word, when the second hand reached the hour. The driver stood beside it in his gloves, with the rear door open and his eyes on the gravel.

She walked toward the gate. She passed him at the distance of an arm. He did not look up, and she did not slow, and the door of the car stayed open behind her for as long as she could hear it, through the gate and down the long road toward the station, in the green and the winter light.

She did not look back at the second-floor window. She was nearly certain that someone in it had not moved.
