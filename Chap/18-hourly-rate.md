# Chapter 18: Hourly Rate

The store was busiest between eleven and midnight and dead after, the kind of dead that made the drink coolers sound louder than they had any right to and turned an hour into something that belonged to nobody.

Itsuki restocked the rice-ball shelf at half past one because it needed doing and because the alternative was standing at the register doing nothing, which he had never learned to be comfortable with. An old man came in at ten to two for a can of black coffee and the sports pages, same as every night Itsuki worked this shift, and said, as he always did, "Still up," like a greeting rather than a question. Itsuki said, "Still up," and that was the whole conversation, and it was somehow enough for both of them.

At a quarter past two a first-year from the neighboring dorm block stumbled in for instant noodles and painkillers, visibly regretting several decisions from earlier in the evening. Itsuki rang him up without comment. Judgment wasn't part of the job, and he remembered, distantly, being nineteen and stupid in exactly that way, back before Koyo had trained most of the stupid out of him and replaced it with something that took longer to regret.

Around three he caught his hand at his face, two fingers pressed to the bridge of his glasses, both hands now, the push he did when the words on a price label stopped holding still. He stood there with it. He knew what it was called. Someone had named it for him over a table in October, with a data point count, and he was annoyed to discover he could no longer do it without hearing her voice say *two-fingered*.

His phone buzzed once against the counter. **Reina:** *Thursday's room has changed to 304. The radiator in 302 has failed again and Yamamoto has declined to be moved.* He typed *noted* and put the phone away before the silence of the store could make more of the message than it was.

___

He did the math on his break, sitting on an overturned crate in the back room with a rice ball the manager let him take, one per shift, for two years, without either of them calling it charity.

The letter was in his bag, soft at the folds now from being carried and reread. *Demonstrated research output, as assessed by faculty evaluation.* He did the arithmetic he had been doing since September, the same figures in a slightly different order: tuition covered, dorm covered, this shift covering the rest, which meant food, the phone bill, the bus fare home at New Year, and a pair of shoes he had been deciding for two months could wait another one.

If the renewal didn't clear, none of that held. He looked at the possibility only as long as it took to name it and set it back down, because there was nothing productive on the other side of looking. There was a floor that gave out, and no net under it except more hours at this register and fewer at that library table, an equation he had already solved enough times to dislike the answer.

He finished the rice ball and went back out front before the break timer told him to.

___

He called his mother a little after four, in the last dead stretch before the commuters, because it was the one hour of the day that reliably belonged to both of them.

She picked up on the second ring. "You're still awake," she said, in the voice she had used since he was small enough to need reminding to sleep, and now used out of habit, because reminding him of anything at four in the morning had stopped working around his second year of high school.

"Working."

"I know. I meant it as a greeting." He could hear a kettle being filled, the clatter of her making tea before a shift that started an hour after his ended, in a city neither of them ever called far away.

"You said nothing urgent."

"I did."

"You've never said that word to me."

A pause. The kettle clicked off. "I wanted to hear your voice," she said. "That's all. Don't make it a thing."

He didn't. He leaned against the shelving with the phone against his ear and looked at the fluorescent light until the spot of it burned a small green ghost across the dark of the window, and let the silence be the answer. She let it too.

"How's the research. The one with the girl."

"She has a name."

"I'm aware she has a name. I'm asking about the research."

"It's going fine. We presented last month."

"Good." The kettle again, and a cupboard. "Work's fine here. Busy. My knees complain more than they used to, but that's November, not anything worth a phone call."

"You should see someone if it's more than November."

"It's more than November most Novembers, and I've never needed to see anyone." Her voice stayed light, the brisk deflection he recognized because he knew where he'd learned it. "Don't start worrying about me from five hundred kilometers. I have plenty of people not worrying about me up close."

"That isn't comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be comforting. It was meant to be true." He could hear the smile in it, the one that meant she knew exactly what she was doing. "I put some aside for the New Year bus. It's not charity. It's a fare."

"I have it."

"I know you have it. I'm telling you it's there."

"Keep it."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. He thought of the fare, and of the arithmetic, and of how her voice had gone flat on purpose, the way his own did when he was tired, which was a thing his father's had done too.

"Go finish your shift," she said. "Eat something that isn't a rice ball."

"It's free."

"It's rice and a piece of fish. Eat a vegetable this decade." A beat, softer. "I'm proud of you. You know that."

"I know."

"Good. Go to work."

She hung up before he had to find anything else to say, which he appreciated, as he appreciated most things she did without being asked.

___

The rush began a little before five, as it always did, and at five, when his replacement came in, the sky had gone the pale grey that wasn't morning yet but had stopped being night. He clocked out, signed the log, and stepped outside into air cold enough to notice, his breath fogging in front of him for the length of the walk.

The streets were nearly empty. A delivery truck idled outside a shuttered restaurant. A single cyclist crossed two blocks up. He took the long way home, the one that passed the rival convenience store and the closed south gate, and did not think about the letter, or his mother's knees, or the girl with a name for exactly one block before he began thinking about all three again.

At the intersection two blocks from the store he stopped for the light. It was red. It was a long red, the kind that stood on a deserted road at five in the morning for nobody, and he stood at the curb with his bag on his shoulder and his hands in his pockets, and his eyes closed.

He opened them to the chirp of the walk signal. He was a half-step off the curb, in the road, one shoe over the white line of the crosswalk, and he could not have said when his feet had moved. There was no car. There was a single bicycle, far up the street, and the light.

He stepped back. He stood there a moment longer than the correction required.

It was, he decided, the hour. It was always this tired by this point in the night. There had never been a reason in two years of walking this route to think of it as anything other than ordinary, and he walked the rest of the way home with the fog of his own breath in front of him and the sound of the signal still in his ears, until the dormitory window went from grey to white and it was late enough to call it morning.
